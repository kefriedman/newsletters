Top Papers This Week

The $34 Trillion Question: Why Can't Europe Build Its Own Apple or Nvidia?

Title: The Value Gap: Europe Cannot Scale | Authors: Bo Becker, Efraim Benmelech, Joao Monteiro

American public companies were worth about a third more than European ones in 2008 — by 2023, the gap had exploded to over 300%, a staggering $34 trillion difference. This isn't just a story of a few U.S. tech giants: the gap is broad-based, concentrated in R&D-intensive and scale-driven industries, and closely tied to the fact that European firm size is capped by home-country GDP while American firms scale freely across a unified market. For anyone debating European competitiveness, capital markets union, or "why doesn't Europe have its own Big Tech," this paper offers the most rigorous accounting yet of exactly where the value is being lost — and it's not where most people think.

Congress Isn't a Meritocracy — It's a Family Business for the Wealthy

Title: Fortunate Sons: Elite Political Selection in American History | Authors: James J. Feigenbaum, Andrew B. Hall, Daniel M. Thompson, Jesse Yoder

By linking over a century of Members of Congress to full-count census records — including their parents and brothers — this paper reveals that America's political class has always been drawn overwhelmingly from economic elites. By 1940, nearly 60% of young MCs held a college degree versus under 5% of comparable men nationally, and 19th-century MCs held roughly triple the wealth of demographically matched peers. Critically, this isn't just about the individuals who make it to Washington — their fathers were richer, more educated, and wealthier too, suggesting deep, persistent structural barriers to political representation. It's a sobering empirical foundation for debates about elite capture, social mobility, and who actually gets to govern.

Macroeconomics

This week's papers tackle big structural questions—why Europe's firms can't scale like America's, whether credit risk actually pays over the long run, and why workers cling to safe jobs even when better ones beckon. There's also a fresh look at inflation dynamics in emerging markets and a clever health-financing experiment from Louisiana.

The Value Gap: Europe Cannot Scale

Authors: Bo Becker, Efraim Benmelech, Joao Monteiro

U.S.-listed firms are now worth roughly $34 trillion more than their European counterparts—a gap that has exploded from a 30% premium in 2008 to over 300% today. The authors show this isn't just a few tech giants skewing the numbers: it's a broad-based pattern concentrated in R&D-intensive, scale-driven industries, and tied to European firms' inability to access equity and venture capital the way U.S. firms can. Why it matters: this is a structural story about capital markets, not just competitiveness rhetoric—and it suggests Europe's growth problem is financial as much as industrial.

Reconstructing a Century of U.S. Corporate Bonds

Authors: Mohammad Ghaderi, Sébastien Plante, Nikolai Roussanov, Sang Byung Seo

Using a new 128-year database of over 100,000 bonds, the authors find that credit risk has historically been meaningfully compensated—a signal that gets muted in modern-era samples, which instead emphasize the term premium. Credit spreads also help forecast future bond returns and macro conditions, though this predictive power weakens once pre-war data is included. Why it matters: much of what we think we know about credit markets is based on a relatively short and unusual postwar sample—long-run data tells a richer story.

Cautious Careers: Job Mobility under Incomplete Markets

Authors: Alex Clymo, Piotr Denderski, Yusuf Mercan, Benjamin Schoefer

Through a clever survey eliciting wage-safety tradeoffs, the authors find that U.S. workers demand a 1.63% pay bump for every extra percentage point of unemployment risk in a new job—evidence that incomplete insurance markets make workers overly cautious job-switchers. Embedded in a general equilibrium search model, this caution has real macro consequences, dampening efficient job-to-job flows. Why it matters: labor market "stickiness" may partly reflect rational risk aversion rather than frictions alone, with implications for how we think about unemployment insurance design.

How Steep is the Phillips Curve in Developing Economies?

Authors: Juan Herreño, Noémie Pinardon-Touati, Malte Thie

Applying a sufficient-statistics approach to India, the authors find the Phillips curve there is an order of magnitude steeper than in the U.S., driven largely by weaker "macro real rigidities" rather than price stickiness itself. Why it matters: this helps explain why inflation in emerging markets responds so sharply to demand shocks, with direct implications for monetary policy calibration in developing economies.

Spending to Save: The Subscription Model for Eradicating Hepatitis C

Authors: Kevin Callison, Rena M. Conti, Jonathan Gruber, Jacob Wallace

Louisiana's Medicaid "subscription" model—paying a flat fee for unlimited access to hepatitis C drugs—tripled treatment volume, cut new diagnoses by 23%, and turned $37 million in extra spending into $266 million in five-year savings. Why it matters: a rare case where an innovative financing structure delivered both better health outcomes and genuine fiscal savings, offering a template for other high-cost drug categories.

Worth a skim: for those tracking longer-run market structure trends, the Becker/Benmelech/Monteiro paper on European valuations pairs well with ongoing debates about EU capital markets union.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics papers explore how social forces shape economic behavior—from union organizing and consumption habits to political dynasties—alongside sharp new evidence on labor market frictions and housing policy.

Winning is Contagious: Social Learning and the Dynamics of Union Support

Authors: Ellora Derenoncourt, Arindrajit Dube, Suresh Naidu, Heather Sarsons, Niharika Singh

Combining 60+ years of union election data with a large-scale experiment involving 9,000 low-wage workers, this paper shows that union wins beget more wins—not because of changing economic conditions, but through social learning. Simply telling workers that unions recently won elsewhere boosts their support and their beliefs about coworkers' support, especially when information is otherwise scarce. Why it matters: this helps explain the historically "lumpy" nature of union growth and suggests information campaigns—not just wage or policy shifts—could reignite organizing momentum.

Fortunate Sons: Elite Political Selection in American History

Authors: James J. Feigenbaum, Andrew B. Hall, Daniel M. Thompson, Jesse Yoder

Linking Members of Congress born 1830–1950 to complete-count census records, the authors find that political power has long run in economically privileged families: MCs are far wealthier and more educated than peers, as were their fathers, and about half of this advantage is shared with their brothers. Why it matters: it offers striking long-run evidence that elite political selection is deeply rooted in family economic status, not just individual merit or effort.

Observability and Social Interactions in Consumption

Authors: Tullio Jappelli, Luigi Pistaferri

Using a clever survey experiment embedded in the Italian Survey of Consumer Expectations, the authors show that making an income windfall visible to coworkers lowers how much people spend—but if everyone gets the same bonus, spending rises again. The implied social-interaction effect (a multiplier of 1.2) reveals that consumption isn't just about resources, but about how income shocks are perceived relative to peers. Why it matters: fiscal stimulus and bonus design may work better or worse depending on whether payments are private or shared publicly.

The Displacement Effects of Domestic Outsourcing

Authors: Mayara Felix, Michael B. Wong

Studying Brazil's 1990s outsourcing reforms, this paper finds that when firms outsource, workers are often laid off outright rather than simply transferred to contractors. Displaced security guards found new jobs within five years, but mostly in different occupations, with wages permanently about 12% lower—translating into lifetime losses of over a year's earnings, especially for those who'd worked at high-paying firms. Why it matters: outsourcing's true cost to workers may be badly underestimated by studies that only look at on-site transfers.

Property Taxes and Housing Allocation Under Financial Constraints

Authors: Joshua Coven, Sebastian Golder, Arpit Gupta, Abdoulaye Ndiaye

This paper models how California-style low property taxes lock elderly homeowners in place, squeezing young families out of the housing market. Raising property taxes to Texas levels would lower prices (easing downpayment constraints for young buyers) while raising holding costs for older owners—shifting the generational balance of homeownership. Why it matters: it offers a fresh angle on the housing affordability debate, spotlighting property tax reform as a lever for intergenerational equity.

Also worth a look: AI Financial Advice examines how LLM-generated advice—and gendered prompting—shapes retirement savings behavior; Income Shocks and Intrahousehold Dynamics reports novel findings from the guaranteed income experiment on how cash transfers ripple through households; and Immigrant Earnings Assimilation, 1981–2021 tracks four decades of wage trajectories, separating return migrants from those who stay.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's Econometrics slate is small but pointed, spanning both ends of the field's spectrum: cutting-edge theory for designing better spillover experiments, and a very practical look at how AI agents are reshaping the day-to-day work of econometric coding.

Robust Signal Maximization in Spillover Experiments

Authors: Kirill Borusyak, Peter Hull, Evan M. Munro (NBER)

This paper tackles a classic headache in experimental design: how do you set up an experiment to credibly estimate spillover (network) effects when you're not sure exactly how unobserved noise behaves? The authors derive the treatment-assignment design and a companion "recentered" instrumental-variables estimator that jointly minimize worst-case variance, showing that the planner faces a fundamental trade-off between maximizing spillover "signal" and spreading treatment variation widely enough to identify it. Their approach yields clean, intuitive rules for common cases like clustered exposure, plus tractable approximations for messier real-world networks, including bipartite (e.g., firm-worker or platform) settings.

Why it matters: Researchers designing field experiments with peer or network effects—from village-level interventions to platform A/B tests—now have a principled, robust recipe for getting more statistical power out of the same budget.

AI Agents and Prompt Engineering in Econometric Coding

Authors: Sebastian Galiani, Federico Ariel López, Raul A. Sosa (NBER)

A timely empirical audit of how well large language models actually perform applied econometric coding tasks. The authors benchmark combinations of software (Stata, R, Python), prompting style (zero-shot vs. few-shot), and "agency" level (a chatbot that writes one script vs. an agent that runs and iteratively fixes its own code). The headline result: giving the AI agency to execute and revise its own code boosts task success from 74% to 96%—for about eight extra cents per run—and interestingly, few-shot prompting helps chatbots much more than it helps agents, suggesting the two strategies are substitutes rather than complements.

Why it matters: As AI coding assistants become standard in applied research workflows, this paper offers concrete, cost-benefit-tested guidance on which setup (software, prompting, autonomy) actually delivers reliable econometric code—useful for anyone deciding how much to trust an AI co-author with their regression scripts.

That's a quieter week on the methods front, but both papers punch above their weight: one refines the theoretical frontier of experimental design, while the other quantifies a very real shift in how econometrics gets done day to day. Worth a read whether you're designing your next RCT or debugging your next Stata do-file with an AI pair programmer.
