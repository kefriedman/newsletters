Trump's BLS Chief Firing Cost the Economy Billions in a Single Day

Title: The Value of Reliable Statistics | Authors: Nicholas Bloom, Erica L. Groshen, Duncan Hobbs, Michael R. Strain

When President Trump fired the head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics and claimed the agency's data were "rigged," economic policy uncertainty spiked dramatically across financial markets. Using an event-study design, the researchers estimate this single action imposed billions in economic costs by undermining public trust in official statistics—a cornerstone of modern economic decision-making. The paper provides the first rigorous quantification of how attacks on statistical agencies translate into real economic harm through increased uncertainty for investors, businesses, and households.

AI Reveals the Secret to Explosive Economic Growth—But There's a Catch

Title: When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth? Feedback Loops in Innovation Networks | Authors: Tom Davidson, Basil Halperin, Thomas Houlden, Anton Korinek

As AI labs increasingly use AI to accelerate their own research, economists have identified the precise conditions under which this feedback loop could trigger "explosive" economic growth that far exceeds historical patterns. Their semi-endogenous growth model shows that automating research activates two reinforcing channels: technological feedback loops and economic scale effects that can overcome diminishing returns to innovation. The findings suggest we may be approaching a fundamentally new growth regime, with profound implications for everything from inequality to global economic stability.

School Phone Bans Actually Work—Here's the Surprising Evidence

Title: The Effects of School Phone Bans: National Evidence from Lockable Pouches | Authors: Hunt Allcott, E. Jason Baron, Thomas Dee, Angela L. Duckworth, Matthew Gentzkow, Brian Jacob

The first nationwide study of school phone restrictions finds that lockable pouches substantially reduce student phone use and improve academic outcomes, despite initial increases in disciplinary problems. Using GPS data, surveys, and test scores from schools across America, researchers demonstrate that phone bans work—but the benefits take time to materialize as students and teachers adapt. The research provides crucial evidence for the growing movement of schools implementing phone restrictions, showing that short-term disruptions give way to meaningful educational gains.

Macroeconomics

This week brings fascinating insights into how households actually perceive monetary policy, the economics of AI-driven research automation, and a novel historical analysis of trade patterns during antiquity's end.

Monetary Policy According to Households: Perceptions, Reactions and Channels

Authors: Francesco Grigoli, Damiano Sandri, Yuriy Gorodnichenko, Olivier Coibion

Using survey data from over 25,000 U.S. households, this study reveals a striking disconnect between how economists think monetary policy works and how households actually perceive it. While households do reduce spending when interest rates rise (particularly on durable goods), they expect monetary tightening to increase both borrowing costs and inflation—contrary to standard models where higher rates are supposed to reduce inflation. The research uses randomized information treatments to identify these effects, showing that households respond to higher expected inflation in ways that challenge conventional monetary transmission mechanisms. Why it matters: These findings suggest central banks may need to reconsider how their policies are communicated and understood by the public.

When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth? Feedback Loops in Innovation Networks

Authors: Tom Davidson, Basil Halperin, Thomas Houlden, Anton Korinek

This paper tackles one of the most pressing questions in modern economics: what happens when AI systems begin researching and improving themselves? The authors develop a semi-endogenous growth model showing that explosive economic growth occurs when technological feedback loops (where AI research improves AI research) combine with economic feedback loops (where higher output funds more research) to overcome diminishing returns to ideas. The model provides clean analytical conditions for when this "intelligence explosion" scenario might unfold, with automation potentially offsetting the typical slowdown in innovation productivity. Why it matters: As AI labs increasingly use AI to accelerate their own research, understanding these dynamics becomes crucial for economic policy and long-term growth projections.

Technology Adoption and Optimal Policy

Authors: Fernando E. Alvarez, Francisco J. Buera, Nicholas Trachter

Using advanced Mean Field Games tools, this research identifies the optimal government intervention in markets where firms must pay fixed costs to adopt improving technologies. The key finding is elegantly simple: optimal policy requires exactly two time-invariant subsidies—one correcting market power distortions and another addressing firms' insufficient incentives to adopt new technologies. This result holds even outside balanced growth paths and for any initial distribution of technology gaps across firms. When complementarities are strong, the model aggregates to a Neoclassical Growth Model with an S-shaped production function. Why it matters: The research provides clear policy guidance for governments seeking to accelerate technology adoption without creating unnecessary distortions.

Trade and the End of Antiquity

Authors: Johannes Boehm, Thomas Chaney

This innovative study uses hundreds of thousands of ancient coins from the 4th-10th centuries to reconstruct trade patterns and economic activity during antiquity's end. By estimating how coins diffused along trade routes, the authors recover granular time series showing that Mediterranean trade was disrupted by the Islam-Christianity border, economic activity shifted away from the Mediterranean starting in the 5th century, and real consumption peaked in the Middle East during the 8th century. Why it matters: This creative use of archaeological data demonstrates new methods for understanding historical economic transitions and their geographic patterns.

Microeconomics

This week brings fascinating insights into how AI is reshaping workplaces, new evidence on housing price measurement challenges, and surprising findings about workplace democracy versus managerial control.

The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks

Authors: Kathryn Bonney, Cory L. Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia S. Foster, John C. Haltiwanger, Aditya A. Pande

Using novel Census Bureau data from 2026, this paper reveals that 18% of U.S. firms currently use AI (32% when weighted by employment), with adoption concentrated among large firms in knowledge-intensive sectors like Information and Professional Services. However, even among adopters, usage remains limited—most firms use AI in just three or fewer business functions, primarily Sales and Marketing. Why it matters: This provides the first comprehensive picture of how AI adoption is actually unfolding across the economy, suggesting a gradual rather than revolutionary transformation.

Power to the Personnel? The Impacts of Managerial Discretion vs. Worker Democracy in Employee Recognition

Authors: Namrata Kala, Madeline McKelway

A randomized experiment in Indian firms compared worker-voted recognition programs against manager-chosen awards, revealing a striking trade-off: workplace democracy increased worker attendance, but managerial discretion improved actual productivity. The study found that managers were better at identifying truly productive workers, while democratic voting led to more work-related discussions among employees. Why it matters: This challenges common assumptions about worker empowerment and suggests that different organizational goals may require different decision-making structures.

Rental Prices and the Cost of Living in the United States, 1914–2006

Authors: Ronan C. Lyons, Allison Shertzer

The researchers solve a long-standing puzzle: why official statistics suggested rental prices grew slower than overall inflation for nearly a century. By constructing a methodologically consistent shelter price series, they show that rents actually increased by a factor of 28.4 rather than the much smaller increases implied by official data. The discrepancy stems from evolving methods used to measure shelter costs in the Consumer Price Index. Why it matters: This has major implications for understanding historical living standards and the true burden of housing costs on American families.

The Value of Reliable Statistics

Authors: Nicholas Bloom, Erica L. Groshen, Duncan Hobbs, Michael R. Strain

When President Trump fired the head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics in August 2025 and claimed the data were "rigged," economic policy uncertainty spiked measurably. The authors use this event to estimate that preserving trust in official statistics generates economic benefits of about $25 for every $1 spent on statistical agencies' budgets. Why it matters: This quantifies the previously unmeasured economic value of statistical credibility and institutional trust.

Econometrics & Methods

This week showcases the versatility of modern econometric methods, with researchers tackling everything from immigration policy impacts to AI bias using sophisticated identification strategies.

Labor Market Impacts of ICE Activity in Trump 2.0

Authors: Elizabeth Cox, Chloe N. East

Using variation in enforcement intensity across geographic areas, this study employs event study and difference-in-differences designs to identify the causal effects of heightened immigration enforcement on labor markets. The researchers find that areas with larger increases in ICE arrests experienced reduced work participation among likely undocumented immigrants, with no positive spillovers to U.S.-born workers—in fact, native workers in immigrant-heavy sectors were harmed. Why it matters: This provides rigorous causal evidence on a highly politicized policy issue, demonstrating how careful identification strategies can inform contentious debates about immigration enforcement.

The Effects of School Phone Bans: National Evidence from Lockable Pouches

Authors: Hunt Allcott, E. Jason Baron, Thomas Dee, Angela L. Duckworth, Matthew Gentzkow, Brian Jacob

This ambitious study combines multiple data sources—surveys, GPS pings, test scores, and administrative records—with a staggered difference-in-differences design to evaluate phone restriction policies. The authors find that lockable pouches effectively reduce phone use but create short-term disruption (increased disciplinary incidents, lower well-being) before generating positive effects in later years. The complex pattern of results highlights the importance of tracking dynamic treatment effects over time. Why it matters: This demonstrates how modern econometric methods can handle multiple outcome measures and timeframes to provide nuanced policy insights beyond simple before-and-after comparisons.

Seeing the Goal, Missing the Truth: Human Accountability for AI Bias

Authors: Sean S. Cao, Wei Jiang, Hui Xu

Using financial prediction tasks as a laboratory, this paper reveals how Large Language Models exhibit "purpose-conditioned cognition"—generating biased outputs when they know the downstream use of their predictions. The key econometric insight comes from comparing performance before and after the LLM's knowledge cutoff date, revealing that goal-aware prompting creates in-sample overfitting that doesn't generalize to new data. Why it matters: This introduces a novel identification strategy for detecting AI bias that could become increasingly important as economists grapple with LLMs as both research tools and subjects of study.

These papers collectively showcase how contemporary econometric practice successfully adapts classical identification strategies—difference-in-differences, event studies, and out-of-sample validation—to tackle emerging policy questions and technological challenges. The methodological sophistication evident in combining multiple data sources and handling dynamic treatment effects reflects the field's continued evolution toward more comprehensive and credible causal inference.
