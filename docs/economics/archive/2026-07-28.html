Top Papers This Week

Is Your ESG Portfolio Backfiring? New Research Says Sustainable Investing May Make Polluters Worse

Title: Counterproductive Sustainable Investing: The Impact Elasticity of Brown and Green Firms | Authors: Samuel M. Hartzmark, Kelly Shue

This paper delivers an uncomfortable verdict for the trillion-dollar sustainable investing industry: shifting capital from "brown" to "green" firms may actually increase net environmental harm. The authors find that cutting green firms' cost of capital barely changes their behavior, but raising brown firms' cost of capital pushes them to become dirtier, not cleaner. The culprit is a flawed focus on percentage emissions reductions, which lets already-clean firms coast while starving the companies that most need capital to fund cleanup investments. It's a must-read for anyone in ESG finance, and a serious challenge to how green investment strategies are currently designed.

Fifty Years of Data Show: Universal Child Care Pays Off for Moms Long After the Kids Grow Up

Title: Investing in Mothers? The Long-Run Impact of a Universal Child Care Program on Maternal Work and Income | Authors: Michael Baker, Jonathan Gruber, Kevin S. Milligan

Using decades of data from Quebec's famous universal subsidized child care program, this paper shows the policy's benefits for mothers don't fade once kids age out of preschool — they compound. Maternal employment gains persist for years, and earnings gains eventually grow to twice the size of the initial employment effect, driven by mothers working more hours and climbing further up the career ladder. As debates over federal child care subsidies heat up again in the U.S. and elsewhere, this is some of the clearest long-run evidence yet that investing in child care is really an investment in maternal careers.

Macroeconomics

This week's NBER lineup spans continents and topics—from Eurobond design and global pension shifts to how public R&D spending abroad boosts foreign productivity and how immigration attitudes respond to hard economic numbers. Together, the papers highlight a common theme: macroeconomic outcomes increasingly hinge on cross-border spillovers and the beliefs (or distrust) that shape policy responses.

Who is Afraid of Eurobonds?

Authors: Francesco Bianchi, Qingyuan Fang, Leonardo Melosi, Anna Rogantini Picco

The authors propose splitting euro area fiscal policy into two lanes: a centralized Treasury issuing Eurobonds for countercyclical stabilization, while national governments handle long-run debt sustainability. Their estimated model shows this arrangement acts as an automatic stabilizer, defusing both deflationary and fiscal-stagflation tail risks without needing to suspend national fiscal rules. Why it matters: this offers a concrete blueprint for euro area reform that could reduce crisis-driven improvisation seen in past downturns.

The Effects of U.S. Public R&D on Global Growth

Authors: Gustavo De Souza, Andrew J. Fieldhouse, Karel Mertens, Ishan B. Nath, Valerie A. Ramey

Using four decades of data across 69 countries, this paper finds that a 1% shock to U.S. federal R&D spending raises foreign TFP by about 1% after 12 years—driven mostly by nondefense research and felt most strongly in non-OECD economies. The mechanism appears to be open scientific knowledge diffusion and capital-embodied trade rather than defense spillovers. Why it matters: cuts to U.S. science funding may carry significant, underappreciated costs for global growth, not just domestic innovation.

Immigration and Macroeconomic Outcomes in OECD Countries

Authors: Gaetano Basso, Mitali R. Mathur, Giovanni Peri

Compiling new immigration data for OECD countries since 1990, the authors show that non-OECD-origin, largely high-skilled immigration has surged and driven measurable gains in GDP per worker—mainly through productivity rather than capital deepening. Notably, standard predictors like push factors or immigration policy indices explain little of the variation; large unexpected surges are common. Why it matters: as native populations age and shrink, immigration's productivity boost may be an increasingly vital—if hard-to-forecast—growth lever for advanced economies.

Trading Off Openness: Economic Information and Immigration Policy Preferences

Authors: Cristina Cattaneo, Daniela Grieco, Nicola Lacetera, Mario Macis

In a survey experiment spanning Germany, Hungary, and Italy, respondents shown credible income-gain projections from more open immigration policy became more supportive of it—though the size of income gains needed to shift majority opinion (1.2–1.7%+ annual per-capita gains) exceeds recent historical growth rates. Why it matters: this pairs well with the Basso/Mathur/Peri paper above—even solid evidence of economic benefits from immigration may not be enough to move public opinion at scale.

Global Pension Asset Allocations and Debt Markets

Authors: Ding Ding, Xiang Fang, Bryan Hardy, Karen K. Lewis

Tracking pension funds worldwide, the authors document a broad shift away from fixed income and toward mutual funds and alternative assets, likely driven by the long era of declining interest rates. Why it matters: as major holders of government debt reallocate toward riskier assets, sovereign bond markets could face reduced structural demand going forward.

Also worth a look: D'Acunto and Weber's global survey of 47,000 consumers finds that distrust in official statistics pushes people toward biased, locally-sourced inflation beliefs (w35511); Gao, Xiong, and Yuan uncover a "sentiment catering" dynamic in Chinese mutual funds, where policy beliefs predict returns but sentiment attracts flows (w35528); and a DR Congo field experiment shows progressive property taxation can boost revenue by 56% even in low-capacity settings (w35536).

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics research spans behavioral finance, information transmission, and the unintended consequences of well-meaning policies—from sanctions that backfire environmentally to sustainable investing strategies that may make brown firms browner.

Mental Models of the Stock Market

Authors: Peter Andre, Philipp Schirmer, Johannes Wohlfart

This QJE paper surveys everyone from everyday households to academic finance experts on how they think stock returns are determined. The striking finding: most households naively equate higher expected earnings with higher expected returns, while academics overwhelmingly favor market efficiency—and financial professionals are split down the middle. Why it matters: these divergent "mental models" help explain persistent disagreement in return expectations and offer a microfoundation for behavioral asset pricing.

The Transmission of Reliable and Unreliable Information

Authors: Thomas Graeber, Shakked Noy, Christopher Roth

Using clever "telephone game" experiments with economic forecasts passed along via voice messages, the authors show that reliability signals get stripped out during word-of-mouth transmission far more than the underlying claims themselves. As a result, listeners end up believing unreliable information almost as much as reliable information once it's been relayed. Why it matters: this offers a mechanistic explanation for how misinformation spreads and gains credibility through social networks.

The Environmental Costs of Sanctions: Flaring and Venting in Venezuela

Authors: Michele Fioretti, Kavanaugh FitzPatrick, Alessandro Iaria

Combining satellite data with production records, this paper finds that U.S. sanctions on Venezuela caused per-barrel gas flaring to rise 2.5x even as output fell by two-thirds—environmental damage far exceeding what proportional output declines would predict. The culprit: surviving oil fields run infrastructure past design capacity while slashing maintenance budgets. Why it matters: a sobering reminder that sanctions can generate substantial unintended environmental costs that policymakers rarely account for.

Counterproductive Sustainable Investing: The Impact Elasticity of Brown and Green Firms

Authors: Samuel M. Hartzmark, Kelly Shue

Hartzmark and Shue introduce an "impact elasticity" measure showing that lowering green firms' cost of capital barely changes their environmental behavior, while raising brown firms' financing costs makes them meaningfully browner. The implication is that ESG capital-shifting strategies can be net counterproductive for the environment. Why it matters: this challenges the core logic behind much of the sustainable investing industry and suggests policy should target absolute emissions rather than percentage reductions.

Investing in Mothers? The Long-Run Impact of a Universal Child Care Program on Maternal Work and Income

Authors: Michael Baker, Jonathan Gruber, Kevin S. Milligan

Tracking Quebec's landmark universal child care program over the long run, this paper finds enduring positive effects on maternal employment—effects that persist well after children age out of preschool. Earnings gains eventually double the size of the initial employment effect, driven by rising work intensity, wage growth, and occupational upgrading. Why it matters: this is compelling evidence that early childcare investments generate compounding career returns for mothers decades later.

Also worth a look: Joshua Gans's Equity as Tribute offers a sharp theoretical take on why governments taking passive equity stakes in regulated firms may not be the benign "skin in the game" story it appears to be. Meanwhile, Billings, Calder-Wang, and Liu's flood risk disclosure study shows how new information platforms are reshaping housing prices and buyer demographics in flood-prone areas, and Baker et al.'s Retail Betting Markets review provides a timely overview of the booming convergence of sports betting, prediction markets, and options trading.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's papers showcase clever identification strategies at work—from regression discontinuity designs exploiting arbitrary rules in credit scoring to difference-in-differences approaches spanning centuries of Chinese history and decades of American public health policy.

Self-Fulfilling Credit Scores

Authors: Victor Duarte, Julia Fonseca

Using a clever regression discontinuity around a 14-day rule that bundles multiple credit inquiries into one, the authors show that credit scores themselves can causally worsen borrowers' financial outcomes—but only for those with prior derogatory marks. For these vulnerable consumers, a score drop of just five points raises default risk by 3.2 percentage points over two years, meaning at least 13% of the score-default relationship is a self-fulfilling prophecy rather than pure risk prediction. Why it matters: This has major implications for how credit scoring models are built and regulated, especially for financially fragile populations where scores may be creating the very outcomes they're meant to predict.

Elite Capture, Meritocracy and Social Stability: Evidence from the Chinese Imperial Examinations

Authors: Jingjing Chen, Jianlei Han, Shing-Yi Wang, Yongxiang Wang

This fascinating historical DiD study examines Song Dynasty reforms that anonymized exam submissions to fight elite capture. In prefectures with powerful elite families, the reforms significantly boosted commoners' success rates and narrowed achievement gaps—while also reducing social unrest. Why it matters: It's a compelling 1,000-year-old natural experiment on how blind evaluation systems can break entrenched privilege and improve social stability, with obvious echoes for modern debates on meritocracy and anonymized hiring/admissions.

Early Life Healthcare Access and Later-life Mortality

Authors: Hamid Noghanibehambari, Jason Fletcher

Linking Social Security death records to the 1940 Census, the authors use a staggered DiD design around the county-by-county rollout of early U.S. public health departments. Exposure to a County Health Department opening during fetal development or early childhood added 1–4 months to lifespan decades later, with stronger effects for more disadvantaged groups. Why it matters: A striking demonstration of how early-life public health investments generate measurable returns nearly a century later—relevant for current debates on public health funding.

Electronic Reporting and Automated Auditing: Environmental Compliance

Authors: Wayne B. Gray, Ronald Shadbegian, Ann Wolverton

This DiD analysis of the first mandatory online wastewater reporting system finds that automated feedback improved reporting completeness and cut discharges—though it also revealed more violations, particularly among smaller and publicly-owned facilities. Why it matters: As regulators increasingly consider AI-based compliance tools, this paper offers real-world evidence on how automation changes both detection and behavior—sometimes in surprising ways.

Also worth a look: Nothing else crossed the wire this week outside these four, but each offers a nice methodological lesson: RD for score manipulation (Duarte & Fonseca), historical DiD for institutional reform (Chen et al.), and long-run DiD for policy exposure (Noghanibehambari & Fletcher, Gray et al.)—a good reminder of how versatile these workhorse techniques remain across vastly different empirical settings.
