AI Investment Boom Signals 170% Productivity Jump—Or Economic Catastrophe

Title: What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition | Authors: Jessica Wachter, Jonathan Wachter

The five largest U.S. tech firms are set to spend $760 billion on AI infrastructure by 2026—double their 2025 spending. The authors show this massive bet implies AI productivity must increase by 170% to avoid bankrupting these companies, with markets pricing in a 50% annual chance of this transformation occurring within two years. This isn't just about tech stocks—it's about whether we're witnessing the birth of a new economic era or the setup for a spectacular crash that could reshape the entire economy.

150-Year Economics Mystery Finally Solved: When Markets Actually Reach Equilibrium

Title: Tatonnement and Price Setting in General Equilibrium | Authors: Iván Werning, Guido Lorenzoni

One of economics' oldest puzzles—when do markets naturally settle into stable equilibrium—has stumped theorists since the 1870s despite being central to understanding how economies function. This breakthrough paper claims to finally provide the answer, potentially revolutionizing our understanding of market stability and price formation. The resolution could reshape how we think about everything from financial markets to policy interventions, offering new insights into when markets self-correct versus when they spiral out of control.

Sports Betting Legalization is Making America Go Hungry

Title: Wagering the Bread Money: Sports Betting Legalization and Food Sufficiency | Authors: Xiaohui Guo, Lizhong Peng, Chad Meyerhoefer

As states rushed to legalize sports gambling after 2021, working-class families paid an unexpected price: food security. This study reveals that legalization reduces household food sufficiency by 2.1% among non-college-educated adults, translating to over 10% more families struggling to put food on the table. With sports betting now legal in most states and heavily marketed during every game broadcast, these findings suggest America's gambling expansion is creating a hidden hunger crisis that policymakers have largely ignored.

Macroeconomics

This week brings fresh insights on monetary policy effectiveness, political economy, and the macroeconomic implications of AI investment, with several papers offering timely perspectives on current policy debates.

Macroeconomic and Fiscal Consequences of Quantitative Easing

Authors: Tobias Adrian, Christopher Erceg, Marcin Kolasa, Jesper Lindé, Pawel Zabczyk

Using a sophisticated macroeconomic model, the authors find that quantitative easing provides substantial economic stimulus during severe downturns while actually reducing public debt—contrasting sharply with traditional fiscal expansion that increases debt burdens. The research shows QE can be particularly valuable when government debt levels are already high, though the authors caution against its use when the economy isn't in a liquidity trap. Why it matters: This analysis provides crucial guidance for central bank policy as officials debate QE's role amid concerns about inflation and central bank losses.

Do Voters Punish Inflation or Pay Cuts? Inflation and Real Wages in U.S. Elections

Authors: Juan Felipe Riaño, Francesco Trebbi

Analyzing the 2021-2024 inflation episode, this study reveals that voters primarily respond to real wage declines rather than inflation rates themselves when making electoral choices. Using county-level data, the researchers find that declining purchasing power, not higher prices per se, drove Republican electoral gains during this period. Why it matters: This challenges conventional wisdom about inflation's political impact and suggests voters are more economically sophisticated than often assumed, focusing on their actual living standards rather than headline price changes.

What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition

Authors: Jessica Wachter, Jonathan Wachter

The massive capital expenditures by tech giants—$380 billion in 2025, potentially doubling in 2026—imply that markets are pricing in productivity gains from AI of roughly 2.7 times current levels. The authors' economic model suggests these investment levels are only sustainable if AI delivers transformational productivity boosts, with a calibrated 50% annual probability of such breakthroughs occurring. Why it matters: This provides a market-based framework for understanding AI's economic potential and the substantial risks if the technology fails to deliver expected returns.

How Small is Small? Non-linearities in Heterogeneous Agent Models

Authors: Javier Bianchi, Greg Kaplan

This technical but important paper shows that standard linear methods dramatically overestimate the effects of fiscal stimulus because they ignore how spending behavior changes as people move away from borrowing constraints. The research demonstrates that larger fiscal transfers become progressively less effective as they shift households out of high-spending regions. Why it matters: The findings suggest policymakers may be overestimating fiscal multipliers, with significant implications for designing effective stimulus programs.

Microeconomics

This week brings fascinating insights into market behavior and individual decision-making, from fundamental questions about market stability to the unexpected consequences of policy implementation and new technologies.

Tatonnement and Price Setting in General Equilibrium

Authors: Iván Werning, Guido Lorenzoni

This paper tackles one of economics' oldest puzzles: when do markets reach stable equilibrium prices? The authors propose a new framework for understanding market stability that has eluded economists for over 150 years. Their approach offers fresh insights into how prices adjust in interconnected markets and when these adjustments lead to stable outcomes. Why it matters: This foundational work could reshape how we understand market dynamics and inform policy decisions about market intervention.

Incentives, Evidence, and Reminders for Bureaucrats: Overcoming Barriers to Policy Scale Up

Authors: Patrick Agte, Daniel R. Morales, Christopher Neilson, Sebastián Otero, Gautam Rao

Even when policies work in trials, they often fail when rolled out nationwide—and this study shows why. In a nationwide experiment in Dominican Republic schools, only 37% of principals implemented an effective educational program when ordered to, compared to 83% in the original trial. Surprisingly, sharing research evidence and providing financial incentives had no effect on implementation rates. Why it matters: This reveals a critical gap between policy design and execution that could explain why many well-intentioned government programs underperform.

Measuring Housing Quality Using Revealed Preference: A Geographic PageRank Approach

Authors: Alex Bell, Sophie Calder-Wang, Shusheng Zhong

Borrowing Google's PageRank algorithm, these researchers created a new way to measure place quality based on where people choose to move. Their Geographic PageRank method analyzes the full network of migration patterns to rank U.S. counties and metro areas by desirability. The approach successfully identifies areas with better environmental quality and can track changes over time for different population groups. Why it matters: This provides policymakers and researchers with a powerful new tool for understanding regional economic development and housing market dynamics.

Wagering the Bread Money: Sports Betting Legalization and Food Sufficiency

Authors: Xiaohui Guo, Lizhong Peng, Chad Meyerhoefer

The rapid legalization of sports betting across U.S. states has had an unintended consequence: reduced food security among working-class households. The study finds that legalization decreases food sufficiency by 2.1% among working-age adults without college degrees, with effects concentrated during NFL seasons and among households with children. The impact translates to a 10.5% decline in food sufficiency among active bettors. Why it matters: This highlights how seemingly unrelated policy changes can have significant welfare effects on vulnerable populations.

But I Like Doing This! Enjoyable Tasks, Contracting, and Automation

Authors: Joshua S. Gans

When workers enjoy their tasks, traditional economic models break down. This paper explores how worker enjoyment affects employment contracts and automation decisions, showing that firms may automate enjoyable tasks not just to replace workers or achieve scale, but to contain workers' voluntary extra effort. Why it matters: As automation advances, understanding these dynamics becomes crucial for predicting which jobs will be automated and how work arrangements will evolve.

Econometrics & Methods

This week brings fascinating applications of econometric methods to unexpected domains, from smartphone effects on fertility to AI-powered analysis of parental speech patterns.

Is the iPhone Birth Control? Causal Evidence from AT&T's 2007–2011 Carrier Monopoly

Authors: Caitlin K. Myers, Ezekiel Hooper

This clever study exploits AT&T's exclusive iPhone monopoly from 2007-2011 to identify causal effects of smartphone access on fertility rates. Using variation in AT&T's mobile broadband coverage as a natural experiment, the authors find that iPhone access reduced births by 4.5-8.0% among teens and 3.2-6.6% among women aged 20-24. The research employs entropy-balanced Poisson models and synthetic difference-in-differences to address the 22% decline in U.S. fertility rates since 2007 that traditional economic factors can't fully explain. Why it matters: This provides the first causal evidence linking smartphone technology to one of the most significant demographic trends of our time.

Characterizing the File Drawer: Evidence from a Meta-Analysis of Parent-Interventions Around the World

Authors: Peter Bergman, Nat Chowanajin

This methodologically innovative meta-analysis tackles publication bias by jointly modeling both published studies and unpublished trials from registries and other sources across 82 randomized controlled trials spanning 20+ countries. The authors develop a novel joint likelihood function that estimates write-up probabilities conditional on statistical significance and characterizes the "file drawer" of unpublished research. After correcting for publication bias, they find modest but consistent effects of low-cost parental engagement interventions: 0.05 SD for test scores and 0.07 SD for grades. Why it matters: This approach offers a new econometric framework for addressing one of the most persistent problems in evidence synthesis.

Leveraging Artificial Intelligence and Field Experiments to Explore Novel Features of Parental Speech and Foster Child Development

Authors: Julie Pernaudet, John A. List, Arnoldo Müller-Molina, Majid Ahmadi, Imrul Huda, Ajay Sailopal, Dana Suskind

This interdisciplinary study combines field experimental variation with AI signal processing to analyze over 600 hours of parent-child interactions from two Chicago-area experiments. The researchers develop machine learning models to identify acoustic features of parental speech that predict child skill development, moving beyond traditional measures like word count to explore previously unobservable aspects of parent-child communication. Why it matters: This represents a frontier application of AI to econometric identification, potentially revolutionizing how we measure and understand human capital formation in early childhood.

How Frontline Supervisors Shape Priorities: Evidence from Police Lieutenants

Authors: Matthew Gudgeon, Andrew Jordan, Taeho Kim

Using the Chicago Police Department's rotational calendar for identification, this study examines how supervisor characteristics affect organizational outcomes in contexts with competing objectives. The authors document significant dispersion in lieutenant fixed effects and find that teams under Black and Hispanic lieutenants make fewer low-level arrests while improving 911 response times, with supervisors influencing behavior through differential allocation of overtime and awards. Why it matters: This provides rare causal evidence on how middle management shapes public sector performance and resource allocation.
