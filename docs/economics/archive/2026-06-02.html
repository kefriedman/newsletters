Top Papers This Week

The $100 Billion Question: How Much Medicare Fraud Goes Undetected?

Title: Medicare Home Health Fraud: How Much, Where, and Who? | Authors: Liran Einav, Amy Finkelstein, Yunan Ji, Neale Mahoney, Gideon Moore

Using machine learning on prosecution data, researchers estimate that Medicare home health fraud may be far more pervasive than previously known—potentially costing taxpayers tens of billions annually. By training AI models on agencies that were actually prosecuted by federal strike forces, they predict fraud rates across the entire country and identify systematic patterns in who commits fraud and where it occurs. This groundbreaking methodology offers policymakers a roadmap for targeting enforcement resources and could revolutionize how we detect healthcare fraud across all Medicare programs.

Why the Race for Artificial General Intelligence Could End Humanity

Title: The AGI Race and Existential Risk | Authors: Ethan Bueno de Mesquita, Wioletta Dziuda, Mattias Polborn

This chilling economic analysis reveals how competition between AI companies creates perverse incentives to sacrifice safety for speed in developing artificial general intelligence. The researchers model how firms allocate resources between racing to be first versus ensuring their AI systems won't cause human extinction—and find that market fragmentation dramatically increases the risk of doom. With a critical insight about market size thresholds, this paper provides the first rigorous economic framework for understanding perhaps the most important policy challenge of our time.

Elite Colleges Don't Boost Your Kids' Grades—But They Transform Their Social World

Title: Elite Universities and the Intergenerational Transmission of Human and Social Capital | Authors: Andrés Barrios-Fernández, Chris Neilson, Seth Zimmerman

A stunning new study tracking Chilean families across generations reveals that elite universities primarily function as social elevators rather than academic ones. Parents who barely scraped into prestigious programs saw their children gain access to high-status schools and wealthy neighborhoods, but showed no improvement in academic achievement. This research fundamentally challenges assumptions about higher education's role and suggests that elite universities may be more about reproducing social hierarchies than developing human capital—with profound implications for admissions policies and educational inequality.

Macroeconomics

This week's research tackles fundamental questions about economic measurement and government finance, from quantifying the hidden value of data assets to examining the sustainability of federal spending and debt dynamics.

The Missing Value of Data

Authors: Ankit Bhutani, Guillermo Ordoñez, Laura Veldkamp

Data has become a critical economic asset, yet national accounting systems fail to capture its true value because it's intangible and often serves as a form of payment (when consumers trade personal information for free services). The researchers propose measuring data's value based on how well it helps firms forecast revenue, finding that properly accounting for data assets would significantly increase measured GDP. Why it matters: This research suggests our economic statistics may be systematically underestimating the size and growth of modern digital economies.

The Risk-free Rate and the Risk-adjusted Growth Rate

Authors: Stavros Panageas

Using a unique historical example of French government bonds indexed to economic growth, this study directly measures the "risk-adjusted" growth rate and finds it exceeds the risk-free interest rate. This challenges conventional assumptions about whether governments can sustainably roll over debt without running primary budget surpluses, since it suggests the present value of future economic output may be infinite under certain conditions. Why it matters: These findings have profound implications for debt sustainability analysis and fiscal policy design.

The Federal Government's Discretionary Spending

Authors: Karen Dynan, Douglas Elmendorf, Theresa Gullo

Federal discretionary spending (both defense and non-defense) has remained remarkably stable as a share of GDP over three decades, despite lacking explicit targets. However, this stability masks growing fiscal pressures as mandatory spending and interest payments consume larger budget shares. Why it matters: The research suggests official budget projections may be overly optimistic, and discretionary programs will face increasing pressure as debt concerns mount and the structured budget process continues to erode.

Geopolitical Fragmentation, Sovereign Debt, and Dollar Dominance

Authors: Felipe Benguria, Eugenio I. Rojas, Felipe Saffie

As geopolitical tensions rise, countries with dollar-denominated debt but increasing yuan-linked revenues face difficult choices between costly debt service, default, or restructuring into yuan-denominated obligations. The model shows how trade barriers and dollar transaction costs can accelerate this shift, potentially creating liquidity spillovers that strengthen yuan debt markets. Why it matters: This research provides a framework for understanding how ongoing US-China tensions could gradually erode dollar dominance in international finance.

Microeconomics

This week brings fascinating insights into how AI is reshaping markets and productivity, while new research reveals surprising patterns in wage determination and intergenerational mobility.

Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools

Authors: Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, Liyuan Yang

Studying over 100,000 GitHub developers, researchers found that while AI coding tools dramatically increase coding activity—with autonomous agents boosting commits by 180%—these gains diminish sharply up the production chain, falling to just 30% for actual software releases. This pattern supports the "weak-link hypothesis," where productivity bottlenecks prevent task-level improvements from translating into final output gains. Why it matters: This research provides crucial evidence about AI's real-world productivity impact, suggesting that optimizing entire workflows, not just individual tasks, is key to capturing AI's benefits.

Off the Labor Supply Curve: The Zero Employer Size Wage Effect Within Large Firms

Authors: André Diegmann, Steffen Müller, Benjamin Schoefer

Using comprehensive German employment data, this study reveals that the famous employer size wage effect—where larger employers pay higher wages—completely disappears when comparing establishments within the same firm, even across different local labor markets. This finding challenges fundamental assumptions about labor market competition and wage determination. Why it matters: The result suggests that wage premiums at large employers aren't driven by productivity differences or local labor market conditions, but by firm-level policies or rent-sharing mechanisms.

Housing Capital and Intergenerational Mobility in the United States

Authors: Ariel J. Binder, Max Risch, John L. Voorheis

Linking Census data with property and tax records for 3.4 million families, researchers discovered that housing wealth is far more persistent across generations than earnings, with less than half of this persistence operating through children's incomes. The racial wealth gap in housing capital also widens dramatically across income levels, more so than earnings gaps. Why it matters: This research highlights how wealth transmission mechanisms beyond income perpetuate inequality, suggesting that policies focused solely on earnings mobility may miss crucial drivers of intergenerational persistence.

Beyond Exposure: Predicting AI Adoption Based on Comparative Advantage

Authors: Ilse Lindenlaub, Ryungha Oh, Maria Alejandra Rodriguez, Laura Veldkamp

Using German survey data, researchers found that common AI "exposure" measures poorly predict actual workplace AI adoption. They develop a new framework based on comparative advantage that considers not just technical feasibility but profitability—balancing AI productivity gains against user costs and worker wages. Why it matters: This work provides a more nuanced understanding of AI adoption patterns, moving beyond simple automation threat assessments to consider the economic incentives driving real-world implementation decisions.

Econometrics & Methods

This week showcases econometrics tackling some of society's most pressing questions, from measuring the real-world harm of stigma to detecting Medicare fraud using machine learning.

Deadly Stigma

Authors: Manasvini Singh

This groundbreaking study uses a regression discontinuity design to measure something economists have long struggled to quantify: the real-world impact of stigma. By exploiting the arbitrary "obese" BMI cutoff in electronic medical records, Singh finds that patients just above this threshold face significantly higher in-hospital mortality rates despite having virtually identical health profiles to those just below it. The research demonstrates that weight stigma among healthcare providers has deadly consequences, offering the first causal evidence of how social bias translates into measurably worse outcomes in high-stakes settings.

Elite Universities and the Intergenerational Transmission of Human and Social Capital

Authors: Andrés Barrios-Fernández, Chris Neilson, Seth Zimmerman

Using Chilean administrative data and a regression discontinuity design, this study reveals the dual role of elite universities in social mobility. While lower-status students who gain admission do successfully transform their children's social networks—getting them into better schools and higher-status peer groups—the research shows academic achievement remains unchanged. The findings suggest elite institutions primarily facilitate social capital transmission rather than human capital development, ultimately reducing intergenerational mobility by strengthening the link between social connections and economic outcomes.

Medicare Home Health Fraud: How Much, Where, and Who?

Authors: Liran Einav, Amy Finkelstein, Yunan Ji, Neale Mahoney, Gideon Moore

This innovative study tackles healthcare fraud detection by combining Medicare claims data with federal prosecution records to train machine learning models that can identify fraudulent home health agencies. The researchers estimate that 3.4% of Medicare home health spending in 2008—roughly $520 million—came from fraudulent providers, demonstrating both the scale of the problem and the potential for AI-driven solutions in fraud prevention. The methodology offers a template for using administrative data and machine learning to detect fraud across other government programs.

AI for Structural Estimation

Authors: Victor Duarte, Julia Fonseca

This methodological breakthrough promises to revolutionize how economists estimate complex dynamic models by reducing computation time from days to minutes. The authors develop a neural network-based approach that treats prices as parameters and uses AI to approximate the relationship between model parameters and data moments, enabling researchers to solve and estimate equilibrium models in a single step. Why it matters: This could democratize access to sophisticated structural modeling techniques and accelerate empirical research across economics.
