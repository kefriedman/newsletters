Top Papers This Week

Why Your Credit Card Charges 22% (Hint: It's Not Just Defaults)

Title: Credit Card Banking | Authors: Itamar Drechsler, Hyeyoon Jung, Weiyu Peng, Dominik Supera, Guanyu Zhou

Using regulatory data on 550 million monthly accounts—representing 90% of the entire U.S. credit card market—this paper tackles a puzzle that's cost consumers billions: why do credit card rates average 22%, an 18-point spread over the risk-free rate, far exceeding any other consumer loan? Surprisingly, charge-offs (averaging 6%) explain only a fraction of that spread. The real culprits are reward payments and non-interest expenses, revealing that "credit card banking" is less about pure lending and more about a bundled rewards-and-services business that half of American households are unknowingly subsidizing. This is essential reading for anyone trying to understand consumer debt, financial regulation, or why your rewards card costs so much.

Solving 2025's Biggest Trade Mystery: Why Record Tariffs Didn't Crush Imports

Title: Tariffs, Investment, and the Missing Trade Collapse | Authors: Francesco Ferrante, Andrea Prestipino, Andrea Raffo, Michael E. Waugh

U.S. tariffs hit Great Depression-era levels in 2025, yet imports actually rose—defying decades of trade theory. This paper solves the puzzle with an ingenious explanation: an AI-driven investment boom masked what would otherwise have been a severe trade contraction. Without the investment surge, the model shows imports would have fallen 10% and output would have contracted 0.7%—meaning tariffs' true drag on the economy has been hidden by a simultaneous tech boom. This is a must-read for anyone trying to disentangle the real effects of trade policy from the confounding forces of the AI investment wave, with major implications for how we interpret 2025's economic data going forward.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro papers span an unusually wide range—from the welfare economics of animal sentience to demographic shifts reshaping global power—but a strong methodological thread runs through several entries, with new tools for identifying policy effects and structural forces in trade and exchange rates.

The Global Transition – The Impact of Demographics and AI on Economic Power

Authors: Seth G. Benzell, Laurence J. Kotlikoff, Victor Yifan Ye

Using a massive 17-region life-cycle model covering 99% of the world's population, the authors project how aging populations and AI adoption will reshape global economic power over this century. Their framework captures the interplay between demographic decline, automation choices, and fiscal policy, with agents living up to 100 years and facing uncertain lifespans. Why it matters: This offers one of the most comprehensive frameworks yet for understanding how the twin forces of demographic change and AI could redraw the map of global economic influence.

Dynamic Effects of Trade

Authors: Benny Kleinman, Ernest Liu, Stephen J. Redding

This paper synthesizes recent advances showing that the welfare gains from trade are far more complex once you account for capital accumulation and technology diffusion over time, not just static reallocation. The authors highlight how short-run and long-run responses to trade liberalization can diverge substantially, with capital accumulation amplifying shock impacts. Why it matters: Policymakers evaluating trade deals may be underestimating long-run gains—or risks—if they rely on static welfare calculations alone.

Exchange Rates, Structural Change, and Productivity Growth

Authors: Paul Bergin, Woo Jin Choi, Ju H. Pyun

Challenging the conventional view that exchange rate movements are merely transitory, this paper shows empirically that currency undervaluation and reserve accumulation policies can durably reshape manufacturing productivity and supply chain orientation. Their dynamic two-country model identifies specific conditions under which such policies boost long-run productivity and welfare at home. Why it matters: This provides fresh theoretical grounding for the industrial policy playbook used by export-oriented economies like China and South Korea.

Identifying Policy Causal Effects from Rule Changes

Authors: Ed Manuel, Christian K. Wolf

This methodological paper carefully dissects a popular empirical technique—interacted local projections—used to study how macro shocks propagate differently across policy regimes. The authors show that this approach and standard policy-shock identification generally recover different aspects of policy effects: shock-based methods capture transitory rate changes, while regime-comparison methods capture more gradual, persistent policy shifts. Why it matters: Applied macroeconomists studying monetary policy transmission need to know precisely what their estimates represent—this paper provides the roadmap.

Algorithm-Driven SVARs: Navigating the Wilderness of Big Data

Authors: Yucheng Yang, Tao Zha

Rather than letting researchers hand-pick variables for structural VAR models, this paper develops a Bayesian algorithm to select variable sets systematically, with striking results: housing production, not household credit, drives output responses, and corporate credit spreads emerge as a key channel for monetary transmission. Why it matters: This could meaningfully change how economists build and interpret models of monetary policy transmission.

Also worth a look: Anderson, Calomiris & Rhee's historical study of the Panic of 1893 reveals how bank managers' personal liability exposure shaped orderly versus disorderly bank failures—a timely lens on managerial incentives in financial crises. And for the philosophically adventurous, Bilal, Pinardon-Touati & Walsh's provocative welfare accounting of animal sentience pushes the boundaries of what "aggregate welfare" should even measure.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics papers explore the frictions and biases shaping markets—from why credit card rates stay sky-high to how outsourcing government functions can backfire, plus fresh evidence on labor market power and the career costs of parenthood.

Credit Card Banking

Authors: Itamar Drechsler, Hyeyoon Jung, Weiyu Peng, Dominik Supera, Guanyu Zhou

Using data on 550 million monthly accounts covering 90% of the US credit card market, this paper explains why credit card interest rates average 22%—an 18-point spread over short-term rates that dwarfs any other consumer lending product. Surprisingly, charge-offs explain only a small part of the spread; the bigger driver is massive marketing spend used to build pricing power, offset by interchange and reward income. Why it matters: this reframes credit card economics as fundamentally about market power and customer acquisition costs, not just default risk.

Monopsony Makes it Big

Authors: Sydnee Caldwell, Arindrajit Dube, Suresh Naidu

This comprehensive review synthesizes the fast-growing literature on employer market power, showing how search frictions, worker preference heterogeneity, and employer concentration all feed into a single measure: the firm-specific labor supply elasticity. The authors caution that inferring elasticities from wage markdowns (and vice versa) is trickier than it seems once efficiency wages enter the picture. Why it matters: a must-read primer for anyone trying to make sense of the modern monopsony literature and its policy implications for minimum wages and antitrust.

Search and Negotiation with Biased Beliefs in Consumer Credit Markets

Authors: Erik Berwart, Sean K. Higgins, Sheisha Kulkarni, Santiago Truffa

In a massive RCT with over 112,000 Chilean loan seekers, researchers found that borrowers systematically underestimate both the level and dispersion of interest rates. Simply eliciting their beliefs prompted more search and lower rates, while showing them a price-comparison tool based on administrative data spurred more negotiation and better terms—without extra search. Why it matters: cheap informational interventions can meaningfully improve loan outcomes, offering a low-cost policy lever for consumer protection.

The Limits of Government Outsourcing: Property Tax Re-Assessments in India

Authors: Esther Duflo, Rema Hanna, Benjamin A. Olken, Shreya Tandon

In a randomized experiment in Chennai, government tax inspectors nearly doubled the revenue gains from property re-assessments compared to private contractors—and were more accurate too. Why it matters: this challenges the standard efficiency case for outsourcing, suggesting that for some tasks, misaligned incentives make private contractors worse, not better, than public bureaucrats.

Do Children Derail Women from the CEO Track?

Authors: Santiago Campos-Rodríguez, David Neumark

Using Costa Rican administrative and civil registry data, this study finds that childbirth triggers a persistent decline in women's odds of becoming a CEO—with no comparable effect for men, who if anything see a bump. Marriage shows the opposite gender split, boosting men's CEO odds while leaving women's largely unchanged. Why it matters: quantifies a key mechanism behind the corporate gender gap using rare, linked demographic-employment data.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics-adjacent papers showcase the field's two big themes: clever identification strategies (difference-in-differences, matched designs, and randomized experiments) applied to pressing social questions, and a foundational methods paper rethinking how we should design experiments when outcomes are bounded. Together they offer both practical empirical takeaways and a reminder that classical experimental design assumptions deserve scrutiny.

Optimal Experimental Design and Estimation when Potential Outcomes are Bounded

Authors: Peter Hull

This is the methodological highlight of the week. Hull shows that when outcomes are bounded (as with binary outcomes), the textbook approach of balanced randomization plus difference-in-means is not optimal. Instead, worst-case mean-squared error is minimized by simple independent random assignment paired with an unconventional "recentered" regression estimator with no intercept. Why it matters: this could reshape best practices for A/B tests and field experiments with binary or bounded outcomes, especially in smaller samples where the gains are largest.

The Effects of Ads on Beliefs and Implications for Consumer Search

Authors: Jean-Pierre H. Dubé, Ilya Morozov, Franklin She, Anna Tuchman

Using a beautifully designed incentivized experiment in a simulated online store, the authors randomize ad exposure and content to show that display ads shift consumer beliefs about price and quality—even when the ad copy says nothing about either. These belief shifts causally affect search and purchase behavior. Why it matters: it separates "visibility" effects from genuine belief updating, offering a template for using randomized experiments to unpack the black box of advertising's persuasive power.

Teen Suicide and the Outcomes of Surviving Peers

Authors: Emily Cuddy, Janet Currie, Elisa Jácome, Lucy Manly

Using linked South Carolina administrative data and a matched difference-in-differences design, the authors find that exposure to a peer's self-harm death raises the probability of a self-harm diagnosis by nearly 50%. Effects vary sharply by demographics and prior mental health history—some students seek care, others turn to criminal behavior instead. Why it matters: the careful matched design and use of "placebo" causes of death (assault, accidents) strengthens the case for contagion as a causal mechanism, with direct implications for school mental health policy.

The Impact of Suicide Hotlines on Health: Evidence from 988 Implementation

Authors: Benjamin Hansen, Niketana Kannan, Jonathan Zhang

A difference-in-differences design exploiting pre-existing gaps in crisis-line capacity finds that the 988 rollout meaningfully improved call-answering operations—but these operational gains didn't translate into detectable reductions in suicide mortality. Why it matters: precise null results here are policy-relevant, suggesting that improving hotline infrastructure alone may not be sufficient to move mortality outcomes.

Also worth a look: Social Clubs and Political Office by Labonne, Querubín, Rossi, Saiegh, San Miguel, and Satyanath uses staggered entry into Argentina's elite Jockey Club and a difference-in-differences approach to show how elite social networks translate into disproportionate political power—a nice application of DiD to political economy and elite persistence questions.
