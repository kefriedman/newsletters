Top Papers This Week

Who's Really Taxing You? New Study Reveals Tariffs Quietly Inflate "Made in USA" Prices Too

Title: The Anatomy of Tariff Pass-through into Consumer Prices | Authors: Mary Amiti, Sebastian Heise, David Weinstein

Using granular data on the 2025 U.S. tariffs, this paper delivers a striking finding: tariffs don't just raise the price of imported goods—they raise the price of domestically produced goods too. About 26% of the tariff hike passes through to consumer prices, with nearly two-thirds coming from pricier foreign varieties and over a third from the higher cost of imported inputs used by U.S. manufacturers. This is essential reading for anyone trying to understand the real inflationary bite of trade policy, since standard pass-through estimates dramatically understate the true consumer cost.

Your Boss Might Be Secretly Blocking Your Promotion—And Now There's Proof

Title: Talent Hoarding in Organizations | Authors: Ingrid Haegele

Using rich personnel records and survey data from a large firm, this paper delivers the first hard evidence of a phenomenon many workers have long suspected: managers deliberately hide or downplay talented employees to keep them on their own teams. Three-fourths of managers admit to hoarding talent, and the practice intensifies when managers face stronger incentives—like performance pay tied to team output or larger teams where talent is easier to conceal. The findings reveal a serious organizational failure mode with real costs to worker mobility, firm efficiency, and the broader labor market's ability to allocate talent well.

When Fear of Deportation Makes Communities Less Safe, Not More

Title: Community Engagement and Public Safety: Evidence from Crime Enforcement Targeting Immigrants | Authors: Felipe Gonçalves, Elisa Jácome, Emily Weisburst

This paper examines the Secure Communities program, which enlisted local police in immigration enforcement, and finds a troubling unintended consequence: it made Hispanic crime victims significantly less likely to report crimes to police, which in turn led to more offending against them. Because reduced reporting and increased victimization roughly cancel out in the official crime statistics, the harm was effectively invisible in standard crime data—until now. With immigration enforcement policy back in the national spotlight, this rigorous causal evidence on the safety trade-offs of such programs couldn't be more timely.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro lineup spans central bank crisis-fighting tools, the murky world of foreign demand for U.S. Treasuries, and a provocative theory of why China's credit booms keep failing to generate inflation—plus a sobering look at how immigration enforcement backfires on public safety.

Reserves and the Buyer of Last Resort

Authors: Frederic Boissay, Harald Uhlig

This paper rethinks the central bank's role during market freezes, arguing that private intermediaries systematically underhold reserves because they ignore how their liquidity buffers help stabilize the broader system. The authors propose a "market-backstop principle"—a Bagehot's Rule for markets rather than banks—combining state-contingent buyer-of-last-resort interventions with light-touch liquidity requirements. Why it matters: With central banks still debating balance sheet size and composition post-QE, this offers a fresh framework for when and how to intervene in frozen asset markets.

The United States and Its Creditors: Assessing Foreign Demand for U.S. Assets

Authors: Anusha Chari, Gian Maria Milesi Ferretti

Using improved nationality-based data, this paper tracks a major shift in who actually holds U.S. Treasuries: foreign official holdings have fallen sharply over the past decade while private holdings—often routed through financial centers—have surged. Rising U.S. equity valuations, not just borrowing, account for much of the deterioration in America's net external position. Why it matters: As debates rage about "de-dollarization" and foreign appetite for U.S. debt, this paper provides crucial nuance on who is really financing the current account deficit.

Monetary Policy in Mandarin Capitalism

Authors: Jeffery (Jinfan) Chang, Wei Xiong

Why does China's credit keep expanding while inflation stays stubbornly low? The authors argue China's production-oriented monetary regime channels credit into capacity rather than demand—liability growth among industrial firms leads to weaker producer prices, higher leverage, and excess inventories rather than price pressure. They also uncover an asymmetric supply chain effect: downstream borrowing pushes up upstream prices, but not vice versa. Why it matters: This helps explain persistent global disinflationary pressure from Chinese manufacturing and complicates simple quantity-theory predictions for the world's second-largest economy.

Community Engagement and Public Safety: Evidence from Crime Enforcement Targeting Immigrants

Authors: Felipe Gonçalves, Elisa Jácome, Emily Weisburst

Studying the Secure Communities program, the authors find that intensified immigration enforcement caused Hispanic crime victims to report less to police—and offending against Hispanics rose as a result, even though reported crime numbers stayed flat (masking the underlying dynamic). They estimate that a 10% drop in reporting drives a 7.9% increase in offending, the first such elasticity in the literature. Why it matters: This is a striking illustration of how policies aimed at one goal (deportation) can undermine a core public good—safety—through weakened community trust.

The Equilibrium Impact of Credit Frictions

Authors: Timothy J. Besley, Peter John Lambert, Isabelle A. Michalski-Roland, John Van Reenen

Using UK administrative data linked to firm default risk, this paper estimates that relaxing credit frictions could raise aggregate output by 25% and wages by 23%—with most gains coming from capital accumulation rather than reallocation. Notably, credit conditions remain tighter than pre-financial-crisis levels, continuing to dampen productivity growth. Why it matters: A rigorous quantification of how much "growth" is left on the table due to lingering post-2008 credit market dysfunction.

Also worth a look: Random Utility with Unobservable Alternatives tackles a subtle econometric pitfall in choice modeling with hidden outside options, while Incomplete Information and Self-Fulfilling Inflation Dynamics offers a Lucas-meets-Keynes model where sentiment-driven inflation equilibria generate monetary non-neutrality—even when fundamentals are neutral.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics research digs into the hidden frictions inside organizations and markets—from managers quietly sabotaging their own employees' careers to auction designs that pay for conservation that would have happened anyway. Several papers also use clever new data sources (LinkedIn edit histories, satellite land-use imagery, Kenyan pharmacy experiments) to catch behavior that traditional datasets would miss.

Talent Hoarding in Organizations

Authors: Ingrid Haegele

Using personnel records and surveys from a large firm, this paper shows that three-fourths of managers admit to hoarding talented workers to protect their own team's performance—and the incentive gets stronger with performance pay, larger teams, and greater visibility of talent. Quasi-random exposure to hoarding causally deters internal job applications and stalls career progression. Why it matters: this is a rare empirical window into a widely suspected but rarely documented organizational pathology that misallocates talent and could help explain sluggish internal mobility.

Additionality and Asymmetric Information in Environmental Markets

Authors: Karl M. Aspelund, Anna Russo

Linking Conservation Reserve Program auction bids to satellite land-use data, the authors find that three-quarters of marginal auction winners weren't actually going to convert their land anyway—meaning taxpayers are paying for conservation that isn't "additional." They show this stems from adverse selection (low-cost bidders often have low counterfactual land-use value) and propose scoring-rule auctions that incorporate expected land-use impact to fix it. Why it matters: a compelling cautionary tale for the many pay-for-performance environmental programs worldwide that assume low bids equal high social value.

Tying Incentives to Information: Diagnosis-Contingent Contracts and Biased Beliefs in Healthcare

Authors: Maria Dieci, Paul Gertler, Jonathan T. Kolstad, Carlos Paramo

In a cluster-randomized trial across 140 Kenyan pharmacies, tying malaria drug subsidies to a positive diagnostic test result—rather than subsidizing treatment outright—roughly tripled testing rates and sharply cut unnecessary drug use. The design directly targets the gap between patients' biased health beliefs and their true condition. Why it matters: a clean, scalable mechanism for curbing wasteful care driven by mistaken beliefs, with relevance well beyond malaria.

Time Travel on Professional Profiles

Authors: Nicholas Bloom, Gideon Moore, Lisa K. Simon, Caelan Wilkie-Rogers

Using monthly vintages of LinkedIn data, the authors discover that nearly 20% of users retroactively rewrite descriptions of jobs they've already left—edits that cluster tightly around job transitions and increasingly reference AI skills post-2022. Why it matters: a methodological warning for the growing literature using resume/profile data to track skills, plus a novel real-time signal of how workers reposition themselves for the AI era.

Long-Run Effects of H-1B Immigration on the U.S. Economy

Authors: Ran Abramitzky, Leah Platt Boustan, Ahmet Gulek, Jens Hainmueller

Exploiting the 1999–2003 H-1B cap expansion, this paper finds that greater H-1B exposure raised incomes for natives and existing immigrants—concentrated in non-STEM occupations—with gains flowing downstream through supply chains but not upstream, consistent with a productivity effect rather than simple labor supply expansion. Why it matters: timely evidence for the ongoing H-1B policy debate, suggesting skilled immigration's benefits may work through better task execution rather than headline patent counts.

Also worth a look: creative destruction generates asymmetric earnings risk that hits high-income "star" workers hardest (Green, Kogan, Papanikolaou & Schmidt); Card, Cardoso & Silva trace how Portugal's austerity-era pharmacy reforms cut pharmacist pay and created a two-tier wage system; and Joshua Gans offers a provocative theory of how automation can erode workers' sense of meaning even without displacing them.

Econometrics & Methods

It's a quieter week on the Econometrics front, but one standout paper offers a compelling methodological lens on a perennial political economy puzzle: why do voters seem to process the same information so differently depending on their partisan priors?

Ambiguous Attribution: Theory and Evidence

Authors: Ricardo Alonso, Monica Martinez-Bravo, Gerard Padró I Miquel, Carlos Sanz, Silvia Vannutelli (NBER)

This paper tackles a classic identification problem in political economy: when government responsibility is split across multiple layers (e.g., federal vs. regional healthcare systems), how do voters decide whom to credit or blame? The authors build a rational Bayesian updating model and test it with an original survey, finding that voters' partisan attribution patterns—crediting the party they like and blaming the party they dislike—can be fully explained by rational inference under ambiguity, without invoking motivated reasoning or psychological bias.

What makes this paper particularly noteworthy from a methods standpoint is its careful survey design: by directly eliciting beliefs about which government layer is responsible alongside performance assessments, the authors are able to empirically distinguish a rational Bayesian mechanism from behavioral alternatives—a distinction that's often assumed away in the political economy literature rather than tested. Why it matters: This has real implications for how we design accountability studies and interpret survey-based measures of "partisan bias"—phenomena that look identical to motivated reasoning in reduced-form regressions may actually reflect optimal information processing under genuine institutional ambiguity.
