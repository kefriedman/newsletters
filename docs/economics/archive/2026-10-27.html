Top Papers This Week

How a $20 Bag of Imported Snacks Can Teach Local Retailers What to Stock

Title: How New Products Enter Markets: Social Networks, Consumer Imports, and Retailer Learning | Authors: David Argente, Esteban Méndez, Diana Van Patten

Using granular customs records, this QJE paper uncovers a beautiful two-step mechanism behind how new products take root in a market: first, information about a new product spreads consumer-to-consumer through real social networks, and then "pioneer" buyers unknowingly signal demand to local retailers, who use that information to decide what to stock. It's a rare causal demonstration of demand externalities operating through both social and market channels simultaneously. For anyone thinking about trade liberalization, product diffusion, or how globalization actually reaches small retailers, this is a landmark empirical contribution.

Managers Keep Picking the Wrong Incentive Contracts — Even When the Data Is Staring Them in the Face

Title: Beliefs Over Contracts | Authors: Francis Annan, Collin B. Raymond

In a striking field experiment, researchers randomly assigned firms to different "equivalent" incentive pay schemes and compared actual outcomes to what managers predicted. The best-performing contract boosted profits by over 55%, yet managers systematically ranked it below worse-performing alternatives — they're good at avoiding bad contracts but bad at spotting great ones. This has major implications for contract theory and suggests a persistent, costly blind spot in how real firms design incentive pay, leaving significant profit on the table.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro papers tackle the geopolitics of trade and money, the fiscal fault lines of climate risk, and how institutions (from tax authorities to money market funds) adjust—or fail to adjust—to changing incentives.

Optimal Flood Insurance in a Second-Best World: Fiscal Spillovers, Reclassification Risk, and Moral Hazard

Authors: Jonathan Gruber, Adam Solomon

As the U.S. shifts the National Flood Insurance Program toward actuarially fair pricing, this paper shows the reform has unintended consequences: declining coverage pushes more cost onto FEMA disaster aid, and climate uncertainty exposes households to "reclassification risk" that private markets can't easily insure. Why it matters: With climate risk intensifying, getting disaster insurance policy right is a first-order fiscal and welfare issue—and seemingly sensible reforms can backfire without careful design.

The Great Game: A Model of Geoeconomic Competition

Authors: Christopher Clayton, Matteo Maggiori, Jesse Schreger

This paper formalizes great-power economic rivalry, modeling when two hegemons choose "containment"—restricting trade with each other and pressuring third countries to pick sides—versus engagement. Containment emerges when exports are substitutable, economies are less interdependent, and geopolitical stakes are high. Why it matters: As US-China tensions reshape trade and investment flows, this offers a rigorous framework for understanding when decoupling becomes a rational strategic choice rather than just rhetoric.

Tariff Staggering

Authors: Stephanie Schmitt-Grohé, Martín Uribe

Using granular U.S. import data, the authors find tariffs are remarkably sticky—in a typical quarter, 60% don't change at all. Embedding this staggered adjustment into a trade model generates hump-shaped macro responses to tariff shocks and overturns classic neutrality results, since gradual adjustment creates anticipation effects that front-load trade responses. Why it matters: With tariffs back in the policy spotlight, understanding their sluggish, staggered implementation is key to predicting their real economic impact.

Tax Productivity and Economic Development: A Quantitative Macroeconomic Analysis

Authors: Radhika Goyal, Anders Jensen, David Lagakos, Abdoulaye Ndiaye, Yuntian Zhao

Drawing on a new macro-historical database, the authors show tax collection efficiency rises sharply with GDP per capita, and build a growth model where weak "tax productivity" limits a government's ability to fund public capital and transfers. Why it matters: This reframes state capacity—not just tax rates—as a core driver of long-run development gaps between rich and poor countries.

Generative AI, Managerial Expectations, and Economic Activity

Authors: Manish Jha, Jialin Qian, Michael Weber, Baozhong Yang

Mining over 120,000 earnings call transcripts with generative AI, the authors construct an "AI Economy Score" that predicts GDP growth, employment, and wages five-plus quarters ahead—beyond existing survey forecasts. The signal is strongest among historically accurate managers, suggesting it captures real information rather than noise. Why it matters: This is a compelling new tool for macro forecasters and a glimpse of how AI can extract forward-looking signals from unstructured corporate communication.

Also worth a look: Coulibaly & Engel revisit strategic monetary policy in open economies with pricing-to-market, finding currency misalignment matters even for self-interested central banks (NBER w35928). Blanchard, Leigh, and Mishra dissect how sovereign credit ratings deviate from model-implied fundamentals, overweighting debt levels and country-specific biases (NBER w35956). And Bézy, Levy, and McQuade show how a French rent-guarantee program expanded housing access and mobility for vulnerable renters (NBER w35932).

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics papers probe the gap between how we think economic agents behave and how they actually do—from managers misjudging incentive contracts, to AI models predicting household choices, to networks shaping both consumer demand and geopolitics.

How New Products Enter Markets: Social Networks, Consumer Imports, and Retailer Learning

Authors: David Argente, Esteban Méndez, Diana Van Patten

Using customs data linked to Costa Rica's social networks, the authors show new products spread in two stages: first through word-of-mouth among connected consumers, then through retailers who observe "pioneer" buyers and stock accordingly. This demonstrates a concrete mechanism—retailer learning from consumer behavior—behind how demand externalities shape product diffusion. Why it matters: it offers rare causal evidence on how informal information networks, not just advertising, drive market entry for new goods.

Beliefs Over Contracts

Authors: Francis Annan, Collin B. Raymond

In a striking field experiment, the best-performing incentive contract boosted firm profits by 55%—yet managers ranked it as inferior to worse-performing alternatives. Standard textbook contract theory, applied without bias, actually predicted performance better than the managers themselves. Why it matters: this is a rare direct test showing that theory can beat practitioner intuition, with real stakes for how firms design pay.

Training Your Replacement: Worker Incentives and the Pace of Automation

Authors: Joshua S. Gans, Peter Landry

This clever theoretical model examines what happens when firms ask workers to train the AI that will replace them. Because finishing the training job ends their own employment, workers have little incentive to cooperate unless compensated—meaning firms may need to pay a "completion bonus" equal to the worker's foregone rents to get automation done efficiently. Why it matters: as AI-driven automation accelerates, this offers a blueprint for how severance and bonus structures could shape the speed and fairness of job displacement.

Selective Admissions in Nursing Homes: Evidence from a Randomized Audit Study

Authors: Liran Einav, Amy Finkelstein, Jesse Gubb, Neale Mahoney, James Okun

A large-scale audit study sending thousands of fictitious inquiries to nursing homes finds 10-15% lower bed availability for Medicaid patients and those with Alzheimer's—gaps that are actually larger at higher-quality facilities. Notably, the study finds no significant discrimination by race. Why it matters: this isolates financial and clinical "cream-skimming" by providers as a key driver of unequal access to quality long-term care.

Representation, Innovation, and Product Adoption

Authors: Jennifer L. Kao, Tamar Oostrom

Exploiting Medicare's 2000 expansion of clinical trial coverage for elderly patients, this paper shows that lowering the cost of including an underrepresented group sharply increased trials for diseases common among the elderly—especially for previously untested drug-disease combinations. Why it matters: it's a compelling case study in how inclusion in R&D testing populations can unlock innovation that specifically serves those groups.

Also worth a look: Finkelstein and Skinner revisit the "Dartmouth Atlas" puzzle on regional health care variation, now emphasizing provider-level productivity differences. Morten, Magaloni, and Cubillos offer experimental evidence on the returns to H-2A temporary migration, documenting gains for Mexican families alongside mental health costs. And for the AI-curious, Andrews, Aberg, and Shapiro introduce an LLM workflow for testing the sensitivity of empirical results to modeling choices.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics papers tackle a shared theme: methodological humility. From stress-testing synthetic controls to correcting bias in fixed-effects variance estimates, researchers are reminding us that the choices we make behind the scenes can swing results as much as the data itself.

We Just Created More Than 1,000 Synthetic Controls To Study California's $20 Fast Food Minimum Wage

Authors: Jeffrey Clemens, Olivia Edwards, Jonathan Meer, Joshua D. Nguyen

The authors build 1,440 "plausibly defensible" synthetic control specifications to estimate how California's $20 fast-food minimum wage affected employment—and find that while 96% of estimates are negative, the range of magnitudes is enormous depending on researcher choices like donor pool and pre-treatment window. Reassuringly, simpler difference-in-differences estimates land near the center of this sprawling distribution. Why it matters: This is a sobering reminder that synthetic control methods, despite their reputation for rigor, can hide substantial researcher degrees of freedom—transparency and robustness checks matter as much as the method itself.

fevc: Leave-Out Bias-Corrected Variance Decompositions in Stata

Authors: Johannes F. Schmieder

This paper introduces a new Stata command implementing the Kline-Saggio-Sølvsten leave-out correction for AKM-style worker/firm variance decompositions, which are ubiquitous in labor economics for studying wage inequality and sorting. Without this correction, estimated variances overstate true heterogeneity—especially in datasets where worker mobility between firms is limited. Why it matters: With a user-friendly tool now available, applied labor economists have fewer excuses to rely on biased variance estimates when decomposing wage inequality into worker and firm components.

Implications of Policy Interventions on Eliminating Gender Gaps

Authors: Carla Varona Cervantes, Russell Cooper

Since randomly assigning gender is impossible, the authors use a structural model of statistical discrimination with dynamic education and labor supply choices to simulate "gender-blind" hiring policies. They find that while blind applications can eliminate wage gaps in the short run, the policy's effects erode as workers adjust their education investments in response—revealing unintended long-run consequences. Why it matters: This is a nice example of using structural modeling to evaluate counterfactual policies that can't be tested experimentally, with real implications for how we think about "gender-blind" labor market reforms.

The Impact of Silver Loading in ACA Health Insurance Marketplaces

Authors: Coleman Drake, David Anderson, Dylan Nagy, Mark Shepard

Using panel data from 2015-2021 across 12 states, this paper provides the first causal estimates of how "silver loading"—a workaround states adopted after federal CSR subsidy payments ended in 2017—affected Marketplace premiums and enrollment. The findings are timely given that Congress is currently debating whether to reinstate CSR funding, which would effectively reverse this policy. Why it matters: With millions of ACA enrollees' premiums at stake, this causal evidence arrives at a critical moment for health policy debates.

Together, these papers reinforce a theme worth keeping in mind: whether you're running synthetic controls, decomposing wage variance, or evaluating counterfactual policies, the credibility of causal claims often hinges on methodological choices that deserve as much scrutiny as the headline results themselves.
