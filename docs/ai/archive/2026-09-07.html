This Week in AI

This week's headline story is Anthropic's release of Claude Opus 5, a major upgrade for long-running agents and professional coding work. Tools & Repos

khoj — A self-hostable AI second brain that lets you build custom agents, run deep research, and query any local or online LLM against your own docs. Good for practitioners building personal or organizational AI assistants with privacy control.

prompts.chat — A community-driven, open-source collection of prompts you can browse, share, and self-host for your team. Good for prompt engineers and teams standardizing prompt libraries.

zod — A TypeScript-first schema validation library with static type inference, widely used for validating LLM outputs and API payloads. Good for developers building type-safe AI application backends.

siyuan — A privacy-first, self-hosted knowledge workspace designed for humans and AI agents to collaborate on notes and research. Good for teams wanting an AI-integrated alternative to Notion or Obsidian.

Research Worth Knowing

Federated Learning That Actually Works for Regional Personalization

Standard federated learning forces a tradeoff: privacy-preserving training or good regional performance, not both—especially with modern transformer-based models. RegionFed shows how to personalize retail search across different geographic markets without the usual collapse in model quality, making it directly relevant to any team building privacy-conscious, multi-region ML systems.

RegionFed: Federated Learning for Personalized Query Understanding

Catching AI Agents Before They Waste Your Compute

Coding agents often act confidently wrong, and you only find out after an expensive, failed execution. This method uses a small "draft" model to flag likely failures by scoring an agent's output—no access to internals needed—giving teams a cheap early-warning system to cut wasted retries in agentic coding pipelines.

How to Speculate about Uncertainty in Agentic Coding

Bring Back Dropout for Cheaper, Better LLM Training

Dropout quietly vanished from LLM training recipes as models scaled, but this study shows that skipping it costs accuracy and robustness. The authors quantify the tradeoff and show how to reintroduce layer dropout to get faster training and inference with fewer downsides—actionable guidance for teams training or fine-tuning large models.

Don't Drop Dropout: Optimizing Layer Sparsity for LLM Training
