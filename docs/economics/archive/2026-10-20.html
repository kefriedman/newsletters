California's Billionaire Tax Might Chase Away More Money Than It Raises

Title: The Net Present Value of the California Billionaire Tax Act | Authors: Benjamin Jaros, Joshua Rauh

With California's proposed one-time 5% wealth tax on billionaires set for 2026, this paper offers a sobering reality check using Forbes billionaire data, real estate records, and tracked departures. The researchers find that behavioral responses—billionaires relocating or restructuring assets—could wipe out a substantial share of expected revenue, even before accounting for lost income tax collections. It's a timely cautionary tale for any state or country considering wealth taxes as a budget fix, showing how mobile capital and mobile people can undermine even well-intentioned redistribution policy.

France Cut the Workweek to 35 Hours—Here's What Actually Happened to Jobs and Output

Title: The Effects of a Shorter Workweek on Employment, Output, and Productivity | Authors: Pauline Carry, Claire Montialoux, Elio Nimier-David, Alexandra Roulet, Nina Roussille

Using rich matched employer-employee data from France's famous shift from a 39- to 35-hour workweek, this study delivers a nuanced verdict on one of labor economics' most debated policy experiments. While aggregate employment barely moved, that headline number masks significant underlying shifts in productivity, firm behavior, and worker outcomes once the hours cut and accompanying payroll tax cuts are disentangled. As "four-day workweek" proposals gain traction globally, this paper provides rare, rigorous evidence on what pay-preserving hour reductions really deliver—and what they don't.

Macroeconomics

This week's lineup spans the geopolitics of trade restrictions, the financial plumbing linking Fed policy to global markets, and the perennial question of whether America can afford its own healthcare system — proof that macroeconomics is as much about power and institutions as it is about models.

Export Controls and the Economics of National Security

Authors: Jingting Fan, Kyle Handley, Nuno Limão

Using detailed U.S. licensing data and a general equilibrium trade model, the authors show export controls impose real costs on targeted countries — but multilateral coordination matters enormously. Controls backed by allied regimes are equivalent to raising export costs by 15-29 log points more than unilateral U.S. action alone. Why it matters: as the U.S. leans harder on export restrictions against China and Russia, this paper offers a rigorous framework for how much "bite" these policies actually have — and why going it alone is far less effective.

A Model of U.S. Monetary Policy and the Global Financial Cycle

Authors: Rohan Kekre, Moritz Lenel

Kekre and Lenel build a model where U.S. rate hikes don't just affect America — they hit the balance sheets of currency-mismatched global arbitrageurs who are structurally short the dollar, amplifying risk premia worldwide. Their calibration helps explain real-world spillovers of Fed policy onto global asset prices and offers a forward-looking twist: in a higher-rate world, these spillovers may actually shrink. Why it matters: central bankers obsessing over "global financial cycle" effects now have a tighter, quantifiable story connecting Fed decisions to emerging-market turmoil.

Modeling and Evaluating the Labor Market and Fiscal Effects of Immigration

Authors: Michele Battisti, Giovanni Peri, Eleonora Romano

This paper merges labor-market and fiscal channels of immigration into one search-and-matching model calibrated across 17 OECD countries. The punchline: native welfare effects depend heavily on immigrants' skill mix and local tax progressivity, but for recent (2019-2024) immigration patterns, natives gain in 14 of 17 countries studied. Why it matters: it's a rare paper offering country-by-country, mechanism-specific answers in a debate usually dominated by heat rather than light.

Will Medical Care Strangle or Save the Federal Budget?

Authors: David M. Cutler

Cutler revisits America's long-term fiscal trajectory through the lens of healthcare spending, noting that growth has slowed sharply since 2010 after decades of outpacing GDP. He sketches scenarios ranging from expensive new treatments reigniting cost growth to technology-driven savings — but even modest reacceleration could outstrip the government's current tax capacity. Why it matters: with debt sustainability back in the headlines, this is a sobering reminder that healthcare costs — not just entitlements broadly — remain the single biggest wildcard in long-run budget forecasts.

Inside Central Bankers' Calendars: How Do Fed Chairs Allocate Their Time?

Authors: Thomas Drechsel, Dali Tsakadze

A fun, data-rich dive into 20,000+ calendar entries from Bernanke, Yellen, and Powell reveals how Fed leadership spends its days — heavy staff engagement, rising media exposure since Bernanke, and a notable uptick in Powell's time with lawmakers. Why it matters: it's a window into how institutional priorities and political pressures on the Fed have evolved in real time.

Also worth a look: Aneja and Xu (w35903) use a century-old natural experiment — the 1906 Naturalization Act — to show how federal standardization eliminated political cycles in citizenship approvals. And Benjamin et al. (w35925) offer a clever statistical fix for comparing subjective survey responses across people, with surprising implications for how we measure the "cost" of unemployment in life-satisfaction data.

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics lineup spans tax policy, financial intermediation, and resource economics—with a pair of papers exposing how middlemen (loan officers, billionaires' tax advisors) can blunt the intended effects of policy, and striking new evidence on groundwater depletion and shorter workweeks.

The Net Present Value of the California Billionaire Tax Act

Authors: Benjamin Jaros, Joshua Rauh

Using Forbes data and real estate/departure records, the authors estimate California's new one-time 5% wealth tax on billionaires will raise just $28–36 billion—less than a third of the $100 billion official projection. The gap stems largely from behavioral responses like relocation. Why it matters: it's a cautionary case study in how migration elasticities can wreck revenue forecasts for wealth taxes.

Are We Consuming Too Much Groundwater?

Authors: Brian Greaney, Joseph S. Shapiro, Katherine R.H. Wagner

Combining satellite data with a dynamic extraction model across 3,400 global aquifers, the authors find 60–80% are being drained faster than optimal, destroying trillions in present-value welfare. Policies guaranteeing fixed water allocations (common in prior-appropriation systems) tend to make over-extraction worse. Why it matters: a sobering, globally comparable diagnosis of one of the world's least-regulated common resources.

Pricing Power in the Mortgage Market: Evidence from GSE Fee Waivers

Authors: Neil Bhutta, Benjamin J. Keys, Adithya Raajkumar

When Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac waived $1.5 billion in fees meant to help lower-income first-time homebuyers, mortgage intermediaries pocketed over 60% instead of passing savings along. Loan officers with a history of pricing loans expensively passed through less than 20%. Why it matters: shows post-crisis consumer protections haven't eliminated steering incentives in mortgage markets.

The Effects of a Shorter Workweek on Employment, Output, and Productivity

Authors: Pauline Carry, Claire Montialoux, Elio Nimier-David, Alexandra Roulet, Nina Roussille

Studying France's shift from 39 to 35 hours (paired with payroll tax cuts), the authors find the combined reform package left overall employment unchanged—but this masks offsetting forces: the hours cut alone would have shrunk employment 3.8%, fully offset by the tax cuts. Effects were skill-biased, boosting managerial employment while reducing hours elsewhere. Why it matters: a nuanced answer to the "4-day workweek" debate, showing policy bundling matters enormously for outcomes.

Green Subsidies with Demand Distortions

Authors: Susanna B. Berkouwer, Joshua T. Dean

In a field experiment with 2,134 Kenyan charcoal users, usage subsidies for a clean induction stove had zero effect on adoption, while purchase-price subsidies generated large welfare gains—worth $10–12 per subsidy dollar—because credit constraints amplified their effectiveness. Why it matters: suggests upfront price subsidies, not ongoing usage subsidies, may be the better tool for clean-tech adoption in credit-constrained markets.

Also worth a look: Hassan, Kalyani, and Lopez Del Valle trace a 137-year "genealogy" of 13 million patents to show foreign ideas have large causal effects on U.S. innovation; Farboodi, Kondor, and Kurlat model how correlated AI-driven screening errors could fragment credit markets into distinct pricing segments; and Bornstein and Indarte find Medicaid expansions increased credit card borrowing by easing households' self-insurance motive.

Econometrics & Methods

It's a quieter week on the econometrics front, but one standout paper showcases how satellite imagery and machine learning are pushing empirical environmental economics into new territory.

Too Little too Late? Nationwide Carbon Trading in China

Authors: Shuang Zhang, Douglas Almond (NBER Working Paper)

This paper offers the first empirical look at China's nationwide carbon market launched in 2021, which—unlike cap-and-trade systems in the EU or California—is "rate-based," meaning compliance depends on emissions intensity rather than an absolute cap. Using plant-level data derived from satellite imagery and machine learning techniques, the authors find that high-efficiency coal plants actually increased both electricity generation and CO2 emissions by about 5% relative to their less efficient counterparts, with no clear evidence that emission intensity improved across the board. Why it matters: The findings raise serious questions about whether rate-based carbon markets—an increasingly popular policy design in emerging economies—can deliver real reductions in emissions, or whether they inadvertently reward scale over sustainability.

From a methods standpoint, this paper is a nice example of how applied econometricians are increasingly leaning on remote sensing data and ML-based imputation to solve classic measurement problems—in this case, inferring plant-level output and emissions in a setting where official reporting is incomplete or unreliable. The identification strategy (comparing high- vs. low-efficiency plants within a rate-based system) is a clean illustration of using regulatory design features to generate a natural source of variation, a technique likely to see more use as satellite-based "nowcasting" of economic activity becomes mainstream in applied micro and environmental economics alike.

While this was the primary release we're covering today, it's worth keeping an eye out for companion work on cap-and-trade versus rate-based systems, as well as emerging papers combining satellite/ML inference with policy evaluation—an area that's likely to grow quickly given the data infrastructure investments highlighted here.
