Top Papers This Week

Your Paycheck Is Lying to You: The Hidden Chaos Behind "Stable" Jobs

Title: Earnings Instability | Authors: Peter Ganong, Pascal Noel, Christina Patterson, Joseph Vavra, et al.

Using high-frequency administrative payroll data, this landmark QJE study reveals that most U.S. workers — even those in stable, ongoing jobs — experience significant month-to-month swings in earnings that annual income statistics completely obscure. The instability isn't random: lower-income, hourly workers bear the brunt, driven largely by firms cutting or adding hours rather than by workers changing jobs. Critically, this volatility isn't just a statistical curiosity — it translates into real spending shocks and higher job separation rates, suggesting that conventional wisdom about "income risk" badly understates what vulnerable workers actually face. This is essential reading for anyone thinking about safety-net design, labor market policy, or the true nature of economic precarity in America.

When Aid Stops, People Die: The Brutal Math of the 2025 US Foreign Aid Freeze

Title: Mortality Effects of the 2025 US Aid Freeze: Evidence from Zambia | Authors: Torsten Figueiredo Walter, Dean Karlan, Niclas Moneke, Ana Radu, Khozya Zyambo

This paper delivers a sobering real-time case study on the human cost of abrupt foreign aid disruptions, exploiting the fact that Zambia's HIV programs were split across two U.S. aid agencies — one that froze activities for months, one that didn't. The result is a rare natural experiment showing immediate drops in HIV prevention services, followed by a cascade of worsening outcomes: more HIV-positive pregnancies, more HIV-exposed births, more opportunistic infections, and ultimately more deaths. With foreign aid budgets under political scrutiny worldwide, this is a timely and rigorous accounting of what's actually at stake when funding gets switched off — even temporarily. It's a must-read for policymakers debating the next aid budget cycle.

Treating Addiction Doesn't Just Save Lives — It Rebuilds Credit Scores

Title: Does Treating Addiction Rebuild Lives? Evidence from Buprenorphine | Authors: Sarah Eichmeyer, Aljoscha Janssen, Jonathan Zhang

Drawing on two decades of Veterans Health Administration data covering over 140,000 patients with opioid use disorder, this study exploits quasi-random physician assignment to show that expanding buprenorphine-experienced clinicians cuts two-year mortality by 3.7 percentage points and doubles long-term medication adherence among overdose survivors. Remarkably, the economic ripple effects are just as striking: instrumented treatment receipt boosts credit scores by 22%, suggesting that addiction treatment doesn't just save lives — it restores people's broader financial and economic functioning. As the opioid crisis remains a top policy priority, this paper offers some of the clearest causal evidence yet that medical treatment investments pay off far beyond the hospital walls.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro research digs into the psychology and politics of fiscal policy, and the economic fault lines between the US and China in an increasingly fragmented world.

Mental Models of Fiscal Policy

Authors: Francesco Bianchi, Era Dabla-Norris, Radhika Goyal, Victoria Haver, Salma Khalid (NBER)

Drawing on survey responses from over 27,000 people across 13 countries, this paper shows that people facing identical fiscal conditions form very different expectations about future tax hikes or spending cuts depending on how they reason about the problem. Those using formal economic logic expect stronger adjustment, while those relying on personal experience expect governments to do less. Why it matters: policymakers trying to build public support for fiscal consolidation may need to tailor communication strategies to how different audiences actually think about deficits, not just the numbers themselves.

Chips & Tokens at the Fault Lines: Two Stacks in One Fragmenting World

Authors: Fabio Natalucci, Alessandro Rebucci (NBER)

Using large language models to analyze global earnings calls, the authors find that mutual US-China economic vulnerability—spanning semiconductors, rare earths, and digital finance—was already a visible theme in corporate narratives in 2025. They argue this "mutual assured economic disruption" is actually stabilizing, constraining further decoupling, while digital assets could tip the balance of dollar dominance in either direction. Why it matters: this offers a fresh framework for thinking about geoeconomic fragmentation not as inevitable decoupling, but as a tense equilibrium shaped by tech and finance simultaneously.

Are Republicans and Democrats Different Taxpayers? Evidence From Property Tax Appeals

Authors: Brad C. Nathan, Ricardo Perez-Truglia, Alejandro Zentner (NBER)

Despite widespread belief that Republican homeowners are more likely to contest their property tax bills, administrative data from Dallas County linked to voter files show the partisan gap in appeal behavior is tiny—just 0.29 percentage points—and dwarfed by demographic differences. Why it matters: this is a nice reminder that stated political identity doesn't always predict economic behavior, cautioning against over-reading partisanship into everyday financial decisions.

Together, these papers highlight a common thread: the gap between how people say they think about economic policy and how they actually behave or reason through it—whether forming fiscal expectations, filing tax appeals, or navigating geopolitical risk in corporate strategy.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics papers span from the granular volatility of American paychecks to the geopolitics of oil cartels, with a strong showing of papers tackling how market design—whether for emissions, insurance, or digital currencies—shapes real-world welfare outcomes.

Earnings Instability

Authors: Peter Ganong, Pascal Noel, Christina Patterson, Joseph Vavra, et al.

Using high-frequency administrative data, this QJE paper reveals that most U.S. workers—even those in stable, ongoing jobs—experience significant month-to-month earnings swings that annual data completely obscures. The instability is firm-driven (mostly through hours fluctuations) and falls hardest on lower-income, hourly workers, fueling both spending volatility and job separations. Why it matters: Policy debates about income support and labor market risk may be seriously underestimating the precarity faced by paycheck-to-paycheck workers.

Designing Low Emission Zones: Theory and Evidence

Authors: Nano Barahona, Natalia Fabra, Imelda, Juan-Pablo Montero

This paper shows that Low Emission Zones—unlike carbon taxes—ration access inefficiently, producing a "bang-bang" welfare result: zones should either be maximally large or not implemented at all. Evidence from Madrid shows the policy pushed buyers toward cleaner cars, but the surplus losses to switchers and inframarginal drivers actually exceeded the emissions benefits. Why it matters: As cities worldwide adopt LEZs, this is a cautionary tale about quantity restrictions versus price-based alternatives like congestion pricing.

Five Facts About Stablecoins

Authors: Lin William Cong, Gordon Y. Liao, Eswar S. Prasad, Tony Zhang

Drawing on wallet-level blockchain data from 2020–2025, the authors document that stablecoins now exceed Bitcoin in onchain transaction volume and are increasingly used for everyday, low-value transfers rather than speculation. They also find falling wallet-access costs and a rapidly growing onchain credit market settled in stablecoins. Why it matters: As regulators debate stablecoin frameworks, this is some of the first rigorous evidence on how these instruments actually function as a transaction currency.

Beyond the First Best: Welfare and OPEC Market Power

Authors: John Asker, Allan Collard-Wexler, Charlotte De Cannière, Jan De Loecker

Using granular cost and production data, this paper estimates that OPEC's cartel behavior has generated $2.2 trillion in welfare losses since 1970 under naive assumptions—but a more careful framework accounting for other market distortions tells a more nuanced story about how much of that loss is deadweight loss versus productive inefficiency. Why it matters: A methodological advance for measuring market power anywhere distortions coexist, with major implications for antitrust and energy policy.

Does Treating Addiction Rebuild Lives? Evidence from Buprenorphine

Authors: Sarah Eichmeyer, Aljoscha Janssen, Jonathan Zhang

Leveraging quasi-random physician assignment across 140,000+ VA patients, this study finds that expanding buprenorphine access cuts two-year mortality by 3.7 percentage points and raises credit scores by 22%—suggesting addiction treatment delivers substantial economic, not just health, returns. Why it matters: A rare causal estimate connecting addiction policy directly to financial well-being, with a favorable cost-benefit case for expanding access.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's lineup blends a core methodological contribution on empirical Bayes shrinkage with applied econometrics spanning AI's effect on scientific discovery, emerging market currency pricing, and the health consequences of streetlight conversion—a reminder that good identification strategies show up everywhere, not just in methods papers.

Shrink Responsibly: Empirical Bayes in Economics

Authors: Peter Hull, Michal Kolesár

This is the methodological highlight of the week: a practical guide to empirical Bayes (eB) shrinkage for estimating unit-specific causal effects—think teacher value-added, hospital quality, or neighborhood effects. The authors show that eB shrinkage can improve "compound decisions" like ranking or selecting top performers even when the underlying statistical model is wrong, but caution that some causal summaries are better estimated directly. Why it matters: anyone running noisy regressions to rank people or institutions—a huge share of applied micro work—will want this as a reference for when shrinkage helps and when it backfires.

How AI Impacts the Quest for Knowledge: Evidence from AlphaFold2

Authors: Myra Mohnen, Joshua S. Gans

Using a massive dataset linking 530,471 proteins to experimental records from 2017–2025, this paper asks whether AI prediction tools push scientists toward novel frontiers or toward deepening existing knowledge. The clever identification strategy exploits variation in AlphaFold2's prediction confidence across proteins: where predictions are highly confident, researchers run fewer first-ever experiments on proteins far from known relatives, but more experiments elsewhere. Why it matters: this offers some of the first causal evidence on how AI reshapes the direction—not just the pace—of scientific discovery, a question with big implications for R&D policy.

Long-run Uncovered Interest Parity in Emerging Markets

Authors: Alessandro Rebucci, Sinem Yagmur Toraman, Giorgio Valente, Nicolò Capirone

Using zero-coupon yield curves for emerging market currencies from 2000–2025, the authors confirm that uncovered interest parity fails badly at long horizons for EM currencies even though it holds for G10 currencies—and show that augmenting the standard regression with sovereign CDS spreads largely fixes the puzzle. The correction is strongest for currencies where credit risk is most clearly priced. Why it matters: this gives international macro researchers a parsimonious way to reconcile UIP violations with sovereign risk, with direct implications for currency forecasting and risk premia modeling.

Light Pollution: Effects on Sleep, Work, and Mental Health

Authors: Joan Costa-i-Font, Janet Currie, Soodeh Saberian

Exploiting the staggered rollout of LED streetlights in Burnaby, BC (versus non-adopting Vancouver), the authors use a combination of border discontinuity, within-city rollout timing, and difference-in-differences designs to isolate the causal effect of lighting conversion on sleep. They find LED conversion reduces difficulty sleeping almost immediately, with knock-on improvements in mental health and other outcomes. Why it matters: it's a nice illustration of triangulating multiple quasi-experimental designs to build confidence in a causal estimate—plus a timely finding for urban policy on infrastructure and public health.

Taken together, this week's papers are a good reminder that econometric rigor—whether through clever instruments, shrinkage estimators, or layered identification strategies—remains the backbone of credible empirical work across every subfield of economics.
