Why Pay Equality Might Be Labor's Secret Weapon

Title: How Does Wage Inequality Affect the Labor Movement? | Authors: Barbara Biasi, Zoë Cullen, Julia Gilman, Nina Roussille

Using a clever trio of methods—a vignette experiment with real union organizers, a natural experiment from Wisconsin teacher pay reform, and real-time data from the 2023 Writers Guild strike—this paper delivers a striking verdict: wage inequality erodes collective action. When high-performing workers gain individual bargaining leverage, they defect from solidarity in favor of going it alone, weakening the coalition that unions depend on. At a moment when labor organizing is making headlines from Hollywood to Amazon warehouses, this research offers a rigorous explanation for why some unionization drives succeed while others stall—and suggests that flatter pay structures may be a precondition for worker power, not just a consequence of it.

Wall Street Is Already Pricing In the AI Productivity Boom—Here's How Much

Title: The Macroeconomic Effect of AI: Sizing the Software Engineering Channel | Authors: Alex Blumenfeld, Jonathon Hazell, Chen Lian, Andreas Schaab

Rather than waiting years for productivity statistics to catch up, this paper uses financial markets as a real-time crystal ball, measuring how stock returns respond to AI exposure based on each firm's share of payroll devoted to software engineers. The result: from November 2022 to December 2025, markets have already priced in a substantial jump in the expected value of software engineering output thanks to AI tools like coding copilots. This is one of the first attempts to quantify a specific, measurable channel of AI's macroeconomic impact rather than speculating about it in the abstract—giving economists and policymakers a concrete number to anchor an otherwise speculative debate about whether AI is really transforming the economy yet.

Macroeconomics

This week's NBER lineup tackles the economics of the frontier — AI's macroeconomic footprint, shrinking global fertility, and fragile shipping lanes — alongside sobering new estimates of climate change's true mortality toll.

The Macroeconomic Effect of AI: Sizing the Software Engineering Channel

Authors: Alex Blumenfeld, Jonathon Hazell, Chen Lian, Andreas Schaab

Using stock market data, the authors back out how much AI has boosted expected software engineering productivity since late 2022 — the equivalent of a permanent 32.6% productivity gain in that sector. This translates into a 3.6% (potentially up to 6.5%) increase in the level of GDP. Why it matters: this is one of the first attempts to put a real-time, market-based number on AI's macroeconomic impact, offering a benchmark as the technology continues to diffuse.

Terra Incognita: The Economics of a Shrinking World

Authors: Jesús Fernández-Villaverde, Patrick Norrick

Global fertility has fallen below replacement for the first time in recorded history, driven not by war or crisis but by a steady, country-specific decline concentrated in poorer nations. The authors argue no known policy lever reliably pushes fertility back toward replacement once it falls, since "modernity" itself makes large families expensive and childlessness cheap. Why it matters: this challenges assumptions embedded in growth models, pension systems, and long-run GDP forecasts worldwide.

Global Temperature and Global Mortality

Authors: Adrien Bilal, Diego R. Känzig, Krzysztof Lisiecki

The paper finds that global warming's mortality effects operate mostly through indirect macroeconomic channels rather than direct heat exposure — 1°C of warming raises annual mortality by 2.5 per 1,000 people, 25 times the effect attributable to local heat alone. This pushes mortality's contribution to the Social Cost of Carbon to $400 per ton, a quarter of total estimated climate damages. Why it matters: it suggests standard climate-damage models focused on local heat effects dramatically understate the true human cost of warming.

Shipping to America

Authors: Xiwen Bai, Jesús Fernández-Villaverde, Yiliang Li, Ricardo Marto, Francesco Zanetti

Using satellite vessel-tracking data, the authors show that recent shipping disruptions caused 20-40 percentage point utilization losses that began showing up months before visible port congestion. Their general equilibrium model captures how firms' uncoordinated reallocation of a shared fleet amplifies welfare losses from these shocks. Why it matters: it offers a more accurate framework than standard "iceberg cost" trade models for understanding supply-chain shocks and their policy responses.

Here, There, and Everywhere: Remote Work's Impact on Employment in the UK

Authors: Ian Burn, Melissa D. Gentry, Joanna Lahey

Exploiting the pandemic-driven surge in remote work as a natural experiment, the authors find that occupations with bigger increases in work-from-home options saw meaningful employment gains for disabled and older workers — though accompanied by shorter commutes and lower wages. Why it matters: remote work may be a durable tool for boosting labor force participation among groups historically pushed to the margins.

Also worth a look: Diego Känzig and coauthors examine how business cycles shape green vs. non-green innovation, finding green R&D is countercyclical — with implications for the timing of climate subsidies. Oded Galor offers a sweeping synthesis of Unified Growth Theory on the origins of global inequality, while Gordon Liao, Eswar Prasad, and Tony Zhang assess how stablecoins and tokenization may reinforce, rather than erode, dollar dominance.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics research zeroes in on bargaining power and how it's being eroded or reshaped—by wage inequality, technology, and even the fine print of property rights—alongside sharp new evidence on how childhood environment and regulatory design quietly shape long-run outcomes.

How Does Wage Inequality Affect the Labor Movement?

Authors: Barbara Biasi, Zoë Cullen, Julia Gilman, Nina Roussille

Combining a vignette experiment, a Wisconsin teacher-pay natural experiment, and real-time data from the 2023 WGA strike, this paper shows that rising wage inequality weakens unions from the inside. High-bargaining-power workers defect toward individual deals, while organizers strategically adjust tactics to retain membership—often at the cost of redistribution. Why it matters: as within-firm inequality grows, this helps explain the broader decline of collective bargaining power in the U.S.

The Commoditization of Labor

Authors: Masao Fukui, Emi Nakamura, Jón Steinsson

Technology that simplifies jobs boosts productivity but also makes workers easier to replace, eroding their bargaining leverage. The authors build a model showing how this "commoditizing" technical change can explain the puzzling divergence between productivity growth and wage growth, particularly in services. Why it matters: it offers a unifying explanation for stagnant wages amid rising firm markdowns on labor.

Like a Good Neighbor: Childhood Neighbors Influence Occupation Choice

Authors: Michael J. Andrews, Ryan R. Hill, Joseph Price, Riley Wilson

Using linked historical census data on over 10 million children, this study finds that simply living next door to someone in a given occupation raises the odds of entering that same occupation decades later by about 10%. Effects are stronger with more intense or repeated exposure and in tightly connected neighborhoods. Why it matters: it's compelling causal-style evidence that geography of opportunity operates house-by-house, not just neighborhood-by-neighborhood.

Property Rights Uncertainty, Prices, and Speculation: Evidence from China's Housing Market

Authors: Hanming Fang, Jing Wu, Vincent Yao

Exploiting Shenzhen's unusual mix of full- and limited-property-rights housing units sitting side by side, the authors show sales markets price in legal uncertainty—implying investors see a 7-21% chance that even "full" 70-year leaseholds could be challenged, and over 50% odds that limited-rights units get no future legal protection. Rental markets, however, ignore this risk entirely. Why it matters: a clever natural experiment quantifying how insecure property rights get capitalized into asset prices.

Are Guidelines Worth Following? Decision-Making Under Incomplete Evidence

Authors: Jason Abaluck, Leila Agha, David C. Chan Jr

Using atrial fibrillation treatment as a case study, the authors show that medical guidelines often rest on trial evidence that clearly measures benefits but poorly measures harms—leading to over-treatment of patients with high bleeding risk. Robust "maximin" treatment rules could cut bleeding complications by a third while preserving most stroke-prevention benefits. Why it matters: a rigorous framework for decision-making that's applicable well beyond medicine, wherever guidelines are built on asymmetric evidence.

Also worth a look: Anna French & Nicholas Vreugdenhil's Permits as Real Options identifies a novel "permitting channel" of regulatory leakage in Colorado's oil and gas sector; Alvarez, Argente, Cavallo & Lippi's Price Setting During a Currency Changeover uses the euro cash changeover to test menu-cost theories with striking precision; and Herskovic & Segal's Network General Equilibrium as a Threat to Identification delivers a sobering methodological warning that time fixed effects don't fully control for general-equilibrium spillovers in network economies.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's NBER lineup is less about new econometric methods and more about clever identification strategies applied to timely questions—from AI's labor market footprint to intergenerational health spillovers. Instrumental variables and structural dynamic modeling do the heavy lifting across the board.

Impact of Adults' College Completion on their Parents' Mortality: Evidence from the 1993 Korean Higher Education Reform

Authors: Ah-Reum Lee, William H. Dow, Jacqueline M. Torres

Using Korea's 1993 Higher Education Reform as a natural experiment, the authors show that a child's college completion doesn't just benefit the child—it appears to extend the parent's life. Reduced-form estimates suggest a striking 27% reduction in parental mortality when the eldest child was affected by the reform, with IV estimates confirming the causal link runs through college completion itself. Why it matters: this flips the usual "returns to education" framework on its head, suggesting policies boosting educational attainment may generate underappreciated intergenerational health dividends.

The Early Impacts of AI on Employment among Recent College Graduates

Authors: Robert W. Fairlie, Jane Wu

Amid heated debate over whether AI is already displacing entry-level white-collar workers, this paper takes a refreshingly agnostic empirical approach using CPS microdata through summer 2026. The headline finding: despite widespread anecdotal concern, unemployment among recent grads did not spike in summer 2026 relative to prior years' seasonal patterns. Why it matters: this is one of the first rigorous attempts to quantify AI's labor market effects with real-time data, and the null result should temper some of the more alarmist narratives—at least for now.

Injury, Workload, and the Strategic Response of Coaches in the NBA

Authors: Kala Krishna, Sergey Lychagin, Lewis S. McLean

Sports economics meets personnel management: using teammate absences as an instrument for player workload, the authors find that increased effort causally raises injury risk—overturning the negative correlations often reported in sports medicine literature (likely driven by reverse causality). More interestingly, they show coaches actively manage private information about injury risk by resting players preemptively, which raises overall productivity. Why it matters: this is a nice illustration of how managers respond to hidden information about worker capacity, with lessons that extend well beyond basketball to any labor setting where overwork is a hidden risk.

Windows of Opportunity in Early Childhood: A Dynamic Model of Multidimensional Development

Authors: Orazio Attanasio, Raquel Bernal, Michele Giannola, Milagros Nores

This paper brings serious structural firepower to early childhood development, estimating a dynamic model linking health, cognition, and socio-emotional skills from ages 1–6, while treating parental investment as endogenous. The flexible, age-varying technology specification allows the authors to pinpoint specific "windows of opportunity" when interventions have outsized effects on child development. Why it matters: for policymakers designing early childhood programs, knowing *when* to intervene—not just whether to—could substantially improve cost-effectiveness.

Taken together, this week's papers underscore how instrumental variables and structural modeling continue to illuminate causal questions in labor, health, and human capital—proof that clever identification never goes out of style.
