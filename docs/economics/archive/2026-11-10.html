Top Papers This Week

Unions Really Do Pay More — And It's Not Just Who They Hire

Title: Why Do Union Jobs Pay More? New Evidence from Matched Employer-Employee Data | Authors: Pierre‐Loup Beauregard, Thomas Lemieux, Derek Messacar, Raffaele Saggio

Using rich Canadian matched employer-employee data, this QJE paper cracks open a decades-old puzzle: do unionized workers earn more because unions sort into more productive firms, or because unions actually extract rents? The answer is both — unionized firms pay about 15 log points more, with 40% of that gap tied to real productivity differences and the rest reflecting genuine bargaining power. This is some of the cleanest evidence yet on the mechanics of the union wage premium, with direct implications for labor policy debates as union membership rates remain a political flashpoint across North America.

We've Been Targeting the Wrong Poor People All Along

Title: Target What? The Foundations of Poverty Targeting | Authors: Rema Hanna, Emily Breza, Benjamin A. Olken, Abhijit Banerjee, Arun G. Chandrasekhar, Gideon Moore

This paper delivers a bombshell for anti-poverty policy: when you ask 5,500 Indonesian villagers who would benefit most from a cash transfer, their answers look nothing like standard consumption-based targeting. In fact, targeting by consumption alone misses 77% of households that peers identify as having the highest marginal social welfare gain. It's a direct challenge to the implicit assumption behind nearly every transfer program on Earth — that the poorest dollar-for-dollar recipient is always the best one — and could reshape how governments and NGOs design safety nets going forward.

The Monetary Policy Shock Everyone's Been Measuring Wrong

Title: The Main Monetary Policy Shock | Authors: Christoph Boehm, Husang Kim, T. Niklas Kroner

Central bank watchers, take note: this paper proposes a new way to isolate the "main" monetary policy shock by finding the combination of market surprises that best explains actual macroeconomic fluctuations — and the results overturn textbook intuition. Their new shock measure explains far more business-cycle variation than prior approaches, yet a contractionary shock barely moves interest rates while persistently lowering prices and strengthening the currency. If this framework holds up, it could force a rethink of how economists identify and interpret monetary policy shocks across the last 30 years of Fed decisions.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro research digs into the hidden costs of the Federal Reserve's post-2008 policy toolkit and challenges conventional wisdom about what actually moves the economy when the Fed acts.

Unconventional Monetary Policy and Deposit Retention

Authors: Philip Strahan, Tuo Yang

Years of near-zero rates and quantitative easing didn't just flood the banking system with cheap deposits—they quietly reshaped who those depositors are. The authors show that ZLB policy and QE attracted more rate-sensitive, often larger depositors, since compressed rates reduced the payoff from shopping around for yield. The catch: once the Fed normalized policy, banks most exposed to these programs saw their deposits become more volatile and costlier to retain. Why it matters: the legacy of unconventional monetary policy may be a banking sector that's structurally more fragile to deposit flight—a dynamic worth remembering after last year's regional bank turmoil.

The Main Monetary Policy Shock

Authors: Christoph Boehm, Husang Kim, T. Niklas Kroner

This paper upends the textbook story of how monetary policy shocks work. By constructing a "main monetary policy shock" that best explains business-cycle fluctuations, the authors find that a contractionary shock barely moves interest rates at all—instead it lowers prices, strengthens the dollar, tightens financial conditions, and dampens industrial production. Unlike narrower, conventionally identified shocks, this measure explains a meaningful share of real economic variation over the past three decades. Why it matters: if interest rates aren't the primary transmission channel, central bank watchers may be focused on the wrong signals when gauging policy's real economic bite.

(In)Credible Development RCTs: False Discoveries, Low Power, and a Solution

Authors: Qingyuan Chai, Kevin Lang, Liang Zhong

A sobering look under the hood of development economics: analyzing 745 hypothesis tests from top journals, the authors estimate that roughly 30% of published "significant" RCT results are false discoveries, with true power averaging just 40%. At standard significance thresholds, there's a 66% posterior probability that a given published finding is a false positive. Why it matters: this raises hard questions about how much weight policymakers should place on individual RCT results—and makes a strong case for investing more systematically in replication.

Also worth a look: TaskNet, from Jennifer Wang, Andreas Haupt, and coauthors, introduces a novel AI-assisted method for building occupational task inventories directly from worker interviews—a potentially powerful tool for tracking how AI is changing job content in real time, with clear implications for labor economists studying automation and the future of work.

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics roundup digs into who really captures the gains in labor and policy markets—from union wage premiums to AI adoption—while two papers challenge how governments target welfare spending and evaluate costly public programs. There's also a sharp reminder that more information doesn't always change behavior, whether it's taxpayers or shoppers.

Why Do Union Jobs Pay More? New Evidence from Matched Employer-Employee Data

Authors: Pierre‐Loup Beauregard, Thomas Lemieux, Derek Messacar, Raffaele Saggio

Using rich Canadian employer-employee data, the authors decompose the 15 log-point union wage premium and find that 40% reflects higher productivity at unionized firms, while the rest is genuine rent extraction by unions—raising worker pay by roughly 9-11 log points. Why it matters: this helps settle a long-standing debate about whether unions create value or simply redistribute it, with real implications for labor policy debates.

Target What? The Foundations of Poverty Targeting

Authors: Rema Hanna, Emily Breza, Benjamin A. Olken, Abhijit Banerjee, Arun G. Chandrasekhar, Gideon Moore

Surveying over 5,500 Indonesians across 600 villages, the authors find that targeting the poorest by consumption misses 77% of households who would gain the most marginal welfare from a cash transfer. Even community rankings of "who's worst off" miss a quarter of high-value recipients. Why it matters: standard poverty-targeting methods may be fundamentally misaligned with the goal of maximizing social welfare from transfers.

Clearing the Fog: Price Transparency, Market Churn, and Consumer Welfare in Short-Term Rentals

Authors: Ginger Zhe Jin, Liad Wagman, Jingyi Xing

Studying California's new all-in pricing law for Airbnb, the authors find a counterintuitive result: average daily rates actually rose 4.8% even as transparency made consumers more price-sensitive, driven by exits of low-quality, fee-reliant listings and a quality-upgraded surviving market. Why it matters: transparency mandates can reshape market composition in ways that complicate simple "lower prices" predictions—a cautionary tale for regulators eyeing similar disclosure rules elsewhere.

When Food Stamps Became Free: Impacts and Implications of Removing the Purchase Requirement

Authors: Amy Finkelstein, Matthew J. Notowidigdo, Charlie Rafkin

Revisiting the 1979 reform that eliminated SNAP's purchase requirement, the authors find enrollment jumped 25% among poorer households, but they find no detectable improvement in infant health outcomes. Recipients appear to value the free benefit at only 59 cents per dollar of government cost. Why it matters: this sharpens the debate between food stamps as a child-health investment versus a redistributive transfer, with the evidence favoring the latter framing.

Knowledge Without Taxation? Experimental Evidence from a Tax Education Program

Authors: Anders Jensen, Nikhil Kumar, Konstantin Poensgen

A Ghanaian property tax experiment shows that educating taxpayers and officials boosts tax knowledge—which even spreads to untreated neighbors—but doesn't raise actual tax payments. Instead, informed taxpayers become savvier about avoidance and more skeptical of enforcement credibility. Why it matters: information campaigns alone may backfire for state capacity if they expose enforcement weaknesses rather than build compliance.

Also worth a look: the AI as Shared Business Infrastructure paper offers rare firm-level evidence that generative AI adoption boosts small e-commerce sellers' sales; a 20-year Bayesian meta-analysis of poverty Graduation programs finds consistently large consumption and income gains across 49 studies; and a methodological piece on Chicago's ShotSpotter contract makes a compelling case for moving policy evaluation beyond simple significance testing.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics-adjacent research showcases the toolkit in action: a high-dimensional estimation breakthrough in finance, a clever causal identification strategy using a novel environmental metric, and a rigorous difference-in-differences policy evaluation. Together, these papers highlight how modern econometric methods are pushing into asset pricing, climate economics, and health policy.

The Hidden Sharpe Ratio

Authors: Mikhail Chernov, Bryan T. Kelly, Semyon Malamud, Johannes Schwab

This paper tackles a thorny high-dimensional estimation problem: how do you estimate the maximum Sharpe ratio achievable across thousands of assets when standard covariance estimation requires regularization that systematically understates it? The authors develop a new estimator that recovers the "hidden" contribution from small-eigenvalue directions, delivering tight bounds with proper confidence intervals—and show that popular ridge-regularized portfolios fall short of the true maximum by a specific, non-vanishing factor. Why it matters: Applied to U.S. equities, their method suggests the true maximal Sharpe ratio may be far higher than previously estimated (~7.4 annualized), with major implications for how we benchmark "anomaly" returns and evaluate asset pricing models.

The Impact of Climate Change on Fisheries

Authors: Fabio Cevenini, Christopher Costello, Carlo Fezzi

Using 2.5 million spatially explicit observations across European fishing vessels, this paper introduces a clever identification strategy—"Marine Degree Days"—that measures species-specific temperature exposure relative to biological thermal tolerances, rather than relying on blunt average temperature measures. This design-based approach lets the authors isolate the causal effect of ocean warming on fishing revenue while addressing thorny issues like spatial selection (vessels moving to avoid bad conditions) and cross-species ecological spillovers. Why it matters: The methodological innovation here—building an exposure metric grounded in biological thresholds rather than linear temperature trends—offers a template for causal climate-impact studies in other sectors.

Beyond Overdose Prevention: Naloxone Coprescription Laws and the Path to Treatment

Authors: Shin-Yi Chou, Jiarun Li, Bingjin Xue

Exploiting staggered state adoption of naloxone coprescription laws, this difference-in-differences study finds a 275% increase in naloxone prescribing, with effects twice as large under mandatory prescription requirements versus voluntary "offer" provisions. Notably, the authors find no evidence of moral-hazard-driven increases in opioid or benzodiazepine prescribing—instead documenting gains in medication-assisted treatment uptake. Why it matters: This is a clean natural experiment showing that a low-cost regulatory nudge can meaningfully reshape downstream treatment-seeking behavior without the feared side effects.

Also worth a look: All three papers this week illustrate complementary strands of applied econometrics—from the high-dimensional/regularization literature (Paper 1) to design-based causal inference using novel exposure metrics (Paper 2) and policy evaluation via staggered-adoption DiD (Paper 3). Readers interested in methodology should pay particular attention to the Sharpe ratio paper's treatment of regularization bias, which has broader lessons for any high-dimensional covariance estimation problem.
