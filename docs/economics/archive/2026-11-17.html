Top Papers This Week

Germany's Wage Inequality U-Turn: It Wasn't the Minimum Wage After All

Title: Labor Market Tightness and the Reversal of Inequality Trends in Germany | Authors: Christian Dustmann, Martin Friedrich, Carl Gergs, Uta Schönberg

For two decades, Germany was the poster child for rising wage inequality — until 2010, when wages at the bottom suddenly started catching up, years before the country's minimum wage law took effect. This QJE paper makes a compelling case that a historically tight post-recession labor market, not policy, did the heavy lifting: stronger job-to-job mobility and employer competition for workers pushed up pay at the bottom of the distribution. It's a powerful reminder that labor market slack (or the lack of it) can be as potent a lever for inequality as any minimum wage or union contract — a lesson with direct relevance for today's debates over tight labor markets in the US and Europe.

H-1B and OPT Workers Are Squeezing Native Earnings, Borjas Finds

Title: The Labor Market Impact of Temporary High-Skill Immigration Programs | Authors: George J. Borjas

With roughly 1.2 million foreign workers employed in the US through temporary high-skill visa programs like H-1B and OPT, Borjas asks a politically charged question: what's the wage effect on comparable native workers? His answer — slower earnings growth and reduced full-time work for the most exposed native skill groups — lands squarely in the middle of an intensifying immigration policy fight. Crucially, Borjas also shows that many "temporary" workers quietly become permanent stayers, a measurement problem that could bias standard IV estimates used throughout this literature. Expect this paper to be cited heavily (and contested just as heavily) as Washington debates the future of skilled immigration visas.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro lineup zeroes in on inflation from two very different angles—one challenging the core logic of interest rate hikes, the other refining how economists model price-setting during inflationary surges—alongside a timely look at the fiscal cliff facing U.S. states and localities.

Monetary Policy in a Phelps–Winter Economy

Authors: Bruce C. Greenwald, Joseph E. Stiglitz

This provocative paper argues that raising interest rates can actually push prices up rather than down. Using the Phelps-Winter "customer market" framework—where firms care about retaining customers, not just maximizing today's profit—the authors show that higher rates make firms discount the future more heavily, prioritizing short-term gains over customer retention, which leads them to raise prices even as demand falls. Why it matters: if correct, this helps explain why recent rate hikes have struggled to tame inflation, and suggests central banks may be fighting inflation with a tool that partially backfires.

An Odd Approximation of Inflation for State Dependent Pricing Models

Authors: Luca Gagliardone, Mark Gertler, Simone Lenzu, Joris Tielens, Al-Mahdi Ebsim

This paper offers a major technical advance for modeling inflation when firms don't adjust prices continuously but instead make lumpy, state-dependent decisions. The authors show inflation behaves as a cubic function of the average price gap, and build a tractable version that can be estimated with standard DSGE tools while still capturing real-world nonlinearities. Applied to the 2021-22 inflation surge in the US and euro area, the model matches the data well across different shock sizes. Why it matters: this gives policymakers and researchers a sharper, more realistic lens for understanding—and forecasting—inflation surges driven by shifts in pricing behavior rather than just demand.

The Recent and Impending Evolution of US Federal Support for State and Local Governments

Authors: Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan

As pandemic-era federal aid (which pushed intergovernmental grants past $1.2 trillion in 2025) winds down, this paper tracks how state and local budgets are adjusting—and flags looming strain from provisions in the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" designed to restrain future federal support. Why it matters: state and local governments may soon face a one-two punch of expiring stimulus and new federal constraints, with real implications for public services and local fiscal policy nationwide.

Federal Eminent Domain and Interstate Energy Infrastructure: Evidence from History

Authors: Zachary Liscow, Joseph S. Shapiro, Heidi L. Williams

Looking back at the 1947 Natural Gas Act amendment, the authors find that granting federal eminent domain power to interstate pipeline developers caused annual pipeline construction to nearly sextuple, far outpacing growth in unaffected oil and intrastate gas pipelines. Why it matters: with today's debates over permitting reform and energy transmission buildout (think grid expansion for renewables), this is a clear historical data point on how much eminent domain authority can accelerate infrastructure development.

Also worth a look: Das et al. (w36029) use rare longitudinal data from four low- and middle-income countries to directly link childhood test scores to adult earnings—finding sizable payoffs to early learning gains in Peru, India, and Pakistan. And DeCicca, Krashinsky, and Nesson (w36015) offer a novel explanation for the widening gender gap in U.S. political preferences, tracing it back to compulsory schooling laws and their differential effects on men's and women's educational attainment.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics research spans labor markets, behavioral demand, and the political economy of taxation—with a standout QJE paper on Germany's inequality reversal and a clever Lyft field experiment that breaks a core tenet of demand theory.

Labor Market Tightness and the Reversal of Inequality Trends in Germany

Authors: Christian Dustmann, Martin Friedrich, Carl Gergs, Uta Schönberg

After decades of rising wage inequality, Germany saw a sharp reversal starting in 2010—years before its minimum wage took effect. The authors trace this to post-recession labor market tightness, which boosted job-to-job mobility and wage growth for workers at the bottom of the job ladder as they moved from low-paying to high-paying firms. Why it matters: This offers a compelling alternative to minimum-wage-centric explanations of inequality trends and highlights how macro conditions shape distributional outcomes through firm-level sorting.

Irregular Demand: The Anti-Compromise Effect in the Field

Authors: Nicola Gennaioli, John A. List, Matthias Rodemeier, Gregory K. Sun

In a massive field experiment with Lyft (7.2 million riders, 47 million choices), adding an expensive premium ride option increased demand for the *cheapest* existing option and for not buying at all—violating basic regularity assumptions in demand theory. The effect strengthens with repeated exposure and even persists when the premium option isn't visibly displayed, suggesting a memory-based attention mechanism rather than rational inference. Why it matters: This has major implications for how firms design pricing menus and for platforms' ability to manipulate consumer attention via pricing architecture.

The Labor Market Impact of Temporary High-Skill Immigration Programs

Authors: George J. Borjas

Borjas finds that natives in skill groups most exposed to H-1B and OPT workers experienced slower earnings growth and reduced full-time employment. The paper develops a model accounting for "stayers"—temporary workers who remain in the U.S. after their visas expire—showing that standard IV estimates may understate cumulative labor market effects via a "stayer multiplier." Why it matters: With immigration policy debates intensifying, this adds rigorous evidence to contentious questions about high-skill visa programs' effects on native workers.

Gambling for Goals

Authors: Costas Arkolakis, Spencer Y. Kwon, Dominic Russel, Claire Shi, Hyunjoo Yang

Using Korean credit bureau, telecom, and credit card data, the authors show that young people who feel life goals like homeownership are out of reach take on more financial risk—including crypto trading, which spikes during unemployment. This Friedman-Savage-style mechanism links economic pessimism directly to gambling-like financial behavior. Why it matters: It offers a behavioral economic explanation for the rise of retail speculation among younger generations facing affordability crises.

Horizontal Equity of Taxation: Citizen Beliefs and Policy Preferences

Authors: Pierre Bachas, Christopher A. Hoy, Anders Jensen, Mahvish Shaukat

Across 29 developing countries, horizontal tax inequities (unequal treatment of self-employed vs. wage earners) rival vertical inequities in magnitude—and progressive reforms often worsen them. Survey experiments in Pakistan reveal that informing citizens about these inequities raises fairness concerns but doesn't translate into support for reform. Why it matters: This reveals a political economy constraint that could stall tax reform efforts globally.

Also worth a look: Colonnelli et al. (Paper 4) find that impact measurement itself boosts revenue growth and capital raised for social enterprises; Chari, Gamble & Henry (Paper 9) argue infrastructure may be the "last arbitrage" for rich-to-poor capital flows; and Pretnar, Kydland & Kydland (Paper 11) model how negative productivity growth in formal nursing care—not demographics alone—drives rising care costs.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics-adjacent papers showcase the toolkit in action: from a clever heteroskedasticity-based identification strategy to a new Stata command for taming high-dimensional fixed effects, researchers are pushing both methods and applications forward.

fereg: Fast High-Dimensional Fixed-Effects Regressions in Stata

Authors: Johannes F. Schmieder

A genuinely useful contribution for applied researchers: new Stata commands (fereg and feppml) that fit linear and Poisson PML regressions with multiple high-dimensional fixed effects, interacted effects, and group-specific slopes—fast. The plugin intelligently switches between projection, Krylov, and multigrid solvers depending on the fixed-effects structure, and supports multiway clustering out of the box. Why it matters: anyone running large panel or gravity-style regressions with many fixed effects will want this in their toolkit.

Investment and Governance: Through The Lens of Sustainability

Authors: Jitendra Aswani, Roberto Rigobon

Using nearly 4,000 sustainable bond issuances, the authors deploy a heteroskedasticity-based identification strategy—a clever way to extract causal estimates without a clean instrument—to show that a 1% increase in sustainable debt raises the likelihood of adopting sustainable governance by 9.2%, which in turn improves environmental performance by 16%. Notably, the effect holds for non-financial firms but not for financial firms with sustainable loans. Why it matters: it offers a rare causal (not just correlational) look at whether ESG-linked financing actually changes corporate behavior.

The Effects of Years of Health Insurance on the Health of Older Adults

Authors: Yu Liu, Kevin Callison, Robert Kaestner

Using HRS panel data, the authors instrument for duration of insurance coverage with baseline uninsured status interacted with the post-ACA period—a neat IV design exploiting policy timing. Despite the ACA generating roughly one additional year of coverage for previously uninsured individuals, the instrumental-variable estimates show no statistically significant improvement in self-rated health. Why it matters: a well-identified null result that should temper expectations about the short-to-medium-run health payoffs of expanded coverage among older adults.

Crime on ICE: Local Police Behavior and Immigration Enforcement in the United States

Authors: John J. Donohue, Tai Markman, Eric A. Baldwin

Exploiting staggered adoption of federal immigration enforcement authority across U.S. police departments (a design now central to modern applied econometrics), the authors find a 7% increase in arrests for low-level, discretionary offenses with no detectable drop in serious crime. Why it matters: a methodologically careful staggered-adoption design that challenges the public-safety rationale often used to justify these enforcement partnerships.

Learn to Earn: The Long-Term Effects of Foundational Learning

Authors: Tahir Andrabi, Natalie Bau, Jishnu Das, Asim Ijaz Khwaja, Catherine Michaud-Leclerc

Combining two distinct identification strategies—school value-added exposure and a randomized village-level information intervention—this 15-year follow-up study in Pakistan links early-grade learning gains to adult earnings. The convergence of results across two very different empirical designs strengthens confidence in the causal story. Why it matters: a rare long-horizon RCT-plus-quasi-experimental triangulation showing that early learning investments pay off decades later.

Also worth a look: Decoding Success uses difference-in-differences and IV methods to evaluate the federal Reading First phonics program in Texas classrooms.
