Top Papers This Week

Does That Taxi License Actually Protect You? Uber Data Says... Not Really

Title: Occupational Licensing of Uber Drivers | Authors: Jonathan Hall, Jason Hicks, Morris M. Kleiner, Yun taek Oh

Using millions of Uber trips and a clever natural experiment—comparing licensed and unlicensed drivers across the NYC/New Jersey border and around Houston's deregulation—the authors find essentially no evidence that occupational licensing improves safety or service quality. Even with telematics data tracking actual driving behavior and instrumental variable techniques to nail down causality, licensed drivers don't outperform their unlicensed counterparts. This is a bombshell for the $200+ billion occupational licensing industry that affects roughly a quarter of U.S. workers, suggesting many licensing regimes may be protecting incumbents rather than consumers.

Why Do People Love Authoritarians They Think Are Doing Great (But Aren't)?

Title: Where the Grass Seems Greener: Economic Misperceptions and Support for Democracy | Authors: Daron Acemoglu, Nicolas Ajzenman, Guillermo Cruces, Martin Fiszbein, Gaston Garcia Zavaleta, Carlos Molina

In a striking survey experiment with over 11,000 Argentines during the 2023 election, this all-star team finds that support for populist and authoritarian regimes is fueled by wildly optimistic—and factually wrong—beliefs about how well those regimes actually perform economically. When researchers corrected these misperceptions with real data, support for anti-democratic alternatives dropped significantly. As democratic backsliding accelerates worldwide, this paper offers a rare, rigorous glimpse into the psychological mechanics behind the global authoritarian wave—and a potential lever (information) to push back against it.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro papers tackle big structural questions—from why stocks and bonds move the way they do, to whether fiscal deficits can hijack inflation, to who controls the minerals powering the energy transition.

Rate Risk and Rate Insurance

Authors: Olivier Wang

This paper offers an elegant explanation for a longstanding asset-pricing puzzle: why do stocks behave so differently from bonds with similar cash-flow duration? By splitting stock returns into a Treasury-like component and a payoff component, Wang shows that falling rates in bad times act as built-in insurance for stockholders, dampening volatility—but the same mechanism also caps expected returns because rising rates in good times offset payoff gains for investors holding government debt. Why it matters: the framework helps reconcile the positive bond risk premium with the negative stock-bond correlation and sheds new light on the mysterious collapse of the value premium.

Monetary-Fiscal Interactions: A Reappraisal

Authors: George-Marios Angeletos, Chen Lian, Christian K. Wolf, Dalton Rongxuan Zhang

The authors revisit the theoretical case for "fiscal dominance"—the idea that deficits alone can drive inflation—and find it rests on a fragile assumption in standard New Keynesian models: that demand shortfalls can persist forever. Once heterogeneous-agent effects (borrowing constraints, finite horizons) and a realistic return to flexible prices are introduced, the explosive feedback loop disappears, leaving a more modest, classical channel through which deficits matter. Why it matters: this is a serious challenge to a school of thought that has gained traction in policy circles amid high deficits and inflation debates.

To Tax or To Ax? Marginal Tax and Spending Multipliers in Consolidations

Authors: Roberto Perotti, Luca Sala

Using a new "marginal cumulative multiplier" concept and a panel of 16 countries' austerity episodes since 1978, this paper finds that cutting spending is far more costly for output (multiplier of 1.5) than raising taxes (multiplier near zero)—directly contradicting the conventional wisdom that tax hikes are more damaging than spending cuts. Why it matters: with several governments weighing how to consolidate high debt loads, this result flips standard policy advice on its head.

Critical Minerals, Geopolitics, and the Green Transition

Authors: Tomás Domínguez-Iino, Jonathan T. Elliott, Allan Hsiao

Lithium, nickel, and cobalt are geographically concentrated but globally traded, giving a handful of mining countries potential cartel-like leverage over the green transition. This paper quantifies how vulnerable battery supply chains are to policy intervention and estimates how shocks in one mineral market spill into others given batteries' multi-mineral composition. Why it matters: as industrial policy increasingly targets critical minerals, this offers a rigorous look at the risks of resource nationalism slowing decarbonization.

Also worth a look: Olivier Wang aside, market-microstructure fans should check out Lu, Song & Zeng's study of Zelle and deposit demand, which shows how faster payment rails are reshaping bank deposit dynamics and monetary transmission. Historically minded readers may enjoy Bordo and Bastidon's non-linear model of currency dominance, which stress-tests reserve-currency regime shifts from the Gold Standard onward using a novel five-shock database.

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics papers take aim at regulation from multiple angles — from occupational licensing and cardiac care markets to SNAP benefit restrictions — with a common thread of testing whether well-intentioned rules actually deliver on their promises.

Occupational Licensing of Uber Drivers

Authors: Jonathan Hall, Jason Hicks, Morris M. Kleiner, Yun taek Oh

Using trip-level Uber data and clever quasi-random dispatch assignment, the authors compare licensed and unlicensed drivers in NYC vs. New Jersey and around Houston's deregulation. The results are striking: licensing shows no consistent effect on safety or service quality, even though deregulated entrants differ substantially in age and experience from their licensed predecessors. Why it matters: This adds to a growing body of evidence questioning whether occupational licensing protects consumers or simply restricts competition.

The Effects of SNAP Sugary Drink Restrictions on Consumption and Welfare

Authors: Hunt Allcott, Amy Finkelstein, Anna Grummon, Matthew J. Notowidigdo

An all-star team examines the wave of state policies banning SNAP purchases of sugary drinks, finding a meaningful 12.4% reduction in purchases of banned items — despite recipients' ability to simply pay with other funds. However, the restrictions also raised feelings of stigma among SNAP recipients, complicating the welfare calculus. Why it matters: As more states adopt these policies, this paper offers some of the first rigorous evidence on their real-world consumption and welfare tradeoffs.

Stress Testing Cardiac Care Markets through Deregulation

Authors: Daniel R. Arnold, Michael R. Richards, Yashaswini Singh, Christopher M. Whaley

When Medicare expanded options for non-hospital cardiac procedures, the intended pro-competition effect ran into state-level regulatory friction. Where entry did occur, low-cost providers — not expensive hospitals — bore the brunt of business stealing, paradoxically increasing Medicare spending by roughly $2.5 million. Why it matters: A cautionary tale for policymakers: federal deregulation can be blunted or even backfire when it collides with state rules and strategic provider behavior.

The Meritocratic Consensus and Stratification in Higher Education

Authors: Zachary Bleemer, Jesse Rothstein

While stratification of students by test scores across colleges has held fairly steady since the 1960s, the gap in instructional spending between schools serving high- and low-testing students has widened sharply since 1970. The authors argue that admissions factors like legacy and athletics play only a secondary role compared to this growing resource divide. Why it matters: This reframes debates over college access and inequality around institutional funding rather than admissions criteria alone.

Labor Market Power with Worker and Firm Heterogeneity

Authors: David W. Berger, Kyle F. Herkenhoff, Jaehun Jeong, Simon Mongey

This ambitious theoretical paper extends models of labor market monopsony to account for heterogeneity across workers and firms, finding that worker-side heterogeneity explains a striking 78% of variation in firms' wage-setting power. Why it matters: The findings have direct implications for how governments should think about income tax design in imperfectly competitive labor markets.

Also worth a look: Cordova & Schaller show Great Recession labor demand shocks widened racial achievement gaps in schools; Chatterji et al. use LLMs to track how entrepreneur-legislators shape pro-business legislation; and Karabarbounis, Pellegrino & Salomao offer a rich structural model of how financial and trade liberalization reshape global firm activity.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics-adjacent lineup is dominated by cautionary tales about research design—from p-hacking's lesser-known cousin "instrument-hacking" to survival models that quietly break under distribution shift—alongside inventive identification strategies applied to everything from Kansas farmland to Bitcoin wallets.

Instrument Hacking

Authors: Michael P. Keane, Timothy Neal, Patrick Vu

This paper identifies a subtle but pervasive threat to IV research: when researchers test multiple candidate instruments and report only the one with the best first- or second-stage statistics, the resulting IV estimate is systematically biased back toward the OLS estimand—the very thing IV is supposed to avoid. Bias worsens as more instruments are tried, unless the added instruments happen to be genuinely stronger. Why it matters: this is essentially "p-hacking" for causal inference, and it suggests journals and referees should scrutinize instrument-selection practices as closely as they now scrutinize specification searches.

Survival Analysis with Limited Overlap and Censoring Distribution Shift

Authors: Meera Krishnamoorthy, Donna Tjandra, Divya Shanmugam, Amanda E. Kowalski, Jenna Wiens

Standard survival analysis assumes censoring patterns are stable and that uncensored observations represent the censored ones—assumptions that fail when, say, hospitals change policies on scheduled deliveries or prophylactic surgeries. The authors formalize when these violations bias time-to-event predictions and propose diagnostics/corrections for limited overlap and shifting censoring mechanisms. Why it matters: a methodological contribution with direct relevance to health economics and clinical prediction models, where mismeasured censoring can silently corrupt policy-relevant estimates.

The Yeoman's Portfolio: Measuring Historical Risk Preferences Using Crop Choice

Authors: Remy Levin, Daniela Vidart

Combining a structural portfolio-choice model with machine-learning-predicted crop returns from historical agronomic data, the authors recover farmer-level risk preferences in Kansas (1889–1929). More risk-averse farmers borrowed less, skipped Liberty Bonds, leaned on informal risk-sharing, and adopted tractors more slowly. Why it matters: a clever structural/ML hybrid for measuring unobservable preferences in historical data—useful template for any researcher trying to quantify latent traits from observed choices in settings without survey data.

Crypto Capture of Foreign Aid

Authors: Sumit Agarwal, Peiyi Jin, Eswar S. Prasad, Daniel Rabetti

Using the timing of World Bank aid disbursements as a natural experiment, the authors construct a novel forensic measure blending on-chain Bitcoin activity, exchange records, and IP-linked web traffic to detect aid diversion. They find sharp spikes in anonymous crypto activity precisely when aid tranches land, concentrated in newly created wallets. Why it matters: an inventive identification strategy—event-study design applied to blockchain data—for a notoriously hard-to-measure phenomenon (corruption), with big implications for aid oversight.

Do Local Synthetic Opioid Overdose Deaths Affect Provider Prescribing of Buprenorphine?

Authors: Nicole Siegal, Sumedha Gupta, Matthew C. Aalsma, et al.

Exploiting the staggered geographic spread of synthetic opioids, this difference-in-differences study (with propensity score matching) finds that local overdose mortality shocks increase buprenorphine prescribing by 5–17% relative to pre-treatment levels, with no evidence of pre-trends. Why it matters: a solid applied DiD design showing providers do respond to local crisis signals—relevant for targeting opioid treatment policy.

Also worth a look: Galor, Klemp, and Wainstock's "Cultural Evolution" offers a sweeping theoretical framework for cultural diversity's origins, while Atkin and Faber's "The Data Revolution, and Its Uses, in International Trade" is a fascinating bibliometric look at how big data has pushed trade research toward quantitative modeling rather than pure empirics—a trend worth pondering across all applied fields.
