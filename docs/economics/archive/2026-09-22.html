Top Papers This Week

What's Keeping Households Up at Night? Scientists Crack the Code on Inflation Anxiety

Title: Attention to the Macroeconomy | Authors: Sebastian Link, Andreas Peichl, Oliver Pfäuti, Christopher Roth

Published in the prestigious Quarterly Journal of Economics, this study tracks German households and firms from 2020-2024 to answer a deceptively simple question: when do people actually think about inflation? The researchers find that attention to inflation isn't random—it spikes when inflation genuinely matters for people's financial decisions, but past experiences with inflation and energy shocks also leave a lasting imprint on what stays "top of mind." This matters enormously for policymakers: if central banks want their messaging to cut through, they need to understand not just what people think about inflation, but when and why they're paying attention at all.

The $2 Trillion Question: Do Stimulus Checks Actually Work? WWII Veterans Have the Answer

Title: The Macroeconomic Effect of Stimulus Checks: Evidence from Postwar Veterans' Payments | Authors: Joao Guerreiro, Jonathon Hazell, Diego R. Känzig, Ed Manuel

In a clever historical detective story, these researchers exploit a forgotten natural experiment—cash payments to WWII veterans—to settle one of macroeconomics' most contentious debates: do stimulus checks actually boost the economy? The answer is a resounding yes, with a multiplier effect that reaches 1 within six months and exceeds 2 after a year, meaning every dollar sent to households ultimately generated more than two dollars in economic activity. Strikingly, the consumption response follows a slow-building "hump-shape" that standard economic models fail to predict, forcing economists back to the drawing board. With governments worldwide increasingly relying on direct payments during crises, this paper offers crucial, rigorously-identified evidence on whether writing checks to citizens really moves the economic needle.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro research spans the political economy of inflation, the microstructure of interest rate expectations, and the ripple effects of the U.S.-China trade war—offering a timely mix of historical lessons and cutting-edge policy analysis.

Fiscal Populism and Monetary Policy Rules

Authors: Luis I. Jácome, Nicolás E. Magud, Samuel Pienknagura, Martín Uribe

Drawing on six decades of global data, this paper shows that left-leaning populist regimes historically drove central banks to finance government deficits directly—fueling higher inflation. Crucially, countries scarred by this history now run monetary policy differently, reacting more aggressively to inflation expectations even long after the populist episode ends. Why it matters: institutional "muscle memory" from past monetization may explain persistent differences in central bank hawkishness across countries today.

Expectations and the Term Structure of Interest Rates

Authors: Pooya Molavi, Alireza Tahbaz-Salehi, Andrea Vedolin

Moving beyond the rational expectations assumption, this paper derives a model-free way to decompose how long-term yields respond to short-term rates into "expectations" versus "risk premia" components. The authors find that for short- and medium-term bonds, shifting expectations—not risk premia—do most of the work, and they uncover systematic biases in how those expectations are formed. Why it matters: this offers a cleaner lens for interpreting bond market signals, which are central to gauging Fed credibility and future rate paths.

Money and Power: Historical Lessons for Stablecoins and U.S. Dollar Dominance

Authors: Michael D. Bordo, Carolyn Wilkins

Looking back at private money systems in the UK, US, Canada, Sweden, and Switzerland, this paper argues that stable, par-value circulation of private currencies has always depended on institutions—not technology. The authors identify five conditions (credible convertibility, transparent backing, uniform regulation, par clearing infrastructure, and crisis management) that stablecoins would need to meet to scale safely. Why it matters: as stablecoins gain traction, this historical framework offers a concrete checklist for regulators worried about financial stability and dollar hegemony.

The Remains of the Trade: The U.S.–China Trade War and its Aftermath

Authors: Pol Antràs, Adrian Kulesza, Andrea F. Presbitero

This paper tracks how Chinese exports have been redirected away from the U.S. and toward third countries in the wake of tariff escalation, and builds new indices to measure how different countries are exposed—whether through import competition, export competition, or consumer/sourcing gains. The authors show that popular "trade similarity" measures used to proxy exposure are flawed because they ignore shock size and market penetration. Why it matters: as trade wars reshape global supply chains, better tools for measuring spillover effects are essential for policymakers assessing which economies win or lose.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics papers explore how attention shapes economic behavior, from household inflation psychology to corporate innovation strategy — with a strong showing from NBER on incentive design, from wildfire prevention to advertising claims.

Attention to the Macroeconomy

Authors: Sebastian Link, Andreas Peichl, Oliver Pfäuti, Christopher Roth

Using rich German panel data spanning the pandemic inflation surge and subsequent disinflation, this QJE paper shows that whether inflation is "top of mind" depends both on its objective payoff relevance and on people's prior experiences — with past experience mattering more as the inflationary environment intensifies. Having inflation top of mind triggers more information-seeking and shapes expectations. Why it matters: this helps explain why inflation expectations can become unmoored from fundamentals, with real consequences for how central banks should communicate.

Digital (Killer?) Acquisitions

Authors: Florian Ederer, Regina Seibel, Timothy Simcoe

Studying 1,200 startup acquisitions by Big Tech, the authors find innovation typically rises before a deal and continues only if further acquisitions follow — but this pattern reflects selection on commercial viability rather than the acquisitions themselves suppressing innovation. This directly complicates the "killer acquisition" narrative that has driven recent antitrust scrutiny of tech mergers. Why it matters: policy aimed at blocking tech acquisitions may be targeting the wrong mechanism if the underlying driver is selection, not suppression.

Incentivizing Self-Protection From Wildfires

Authors: Patrick Baylis, Judson Boomhower, Bob Horton, Chris Mulverhill

Two randomized field experiments in Oregon show that cash incentives ($100–$500) drove up to 12% of homeowners to schedule free wildfire risk assessments, versus just 3% under information-only outreach. Why it matters: as climate risk grows, this offers a rare, rigorously tested lever for closing the gap between low-cost protective measures and low real-world adoption.

Well-Being Salience in Corporate Disclosures and Worker Health

Authors: Riccardo Di Francesco, Peter Hull, Seetha Menon

Using language models to score how much Danish firms emphasize employee well-being in disclosures, the authors find that workers who move to "high-salience" firms take more sick leave without any corresponding increase in healthcare use — suggesting a behavioral response to workplace culture rather than an actual health decline. Why it matters: it's a clever demonstration that corporate rhetoric about well-being can reshape worker behavior in ways that don't map onto true health outcomes.

The Impact of Fertility on Mothers' Remote Work

Authors: Benjamin W. Cowan, Mohamed Hafis Zida

Exploiting sibling sex composition and twin births as natural experiments, this paper finds that an additional child used to increase mothers' likelihood of working remotely — but this relationship flipped negative after 2020, once remote work became mainstream. Why it matters: it suggests the post-pandemic remote work boom changed who benefits from flexible arrangements, with implications for the future of family-friendly work policy.

Also worth a look: Joshua Gans' theoretical piece on advertising claims and pricing shows how firms strategically use ambiguous evidence to shape consumer beliefs; a natural-gas pricing experiment in the Republic of Georgia offers a rare look at demand response to genuinely free utilities; and new evidence on the 2017 TCJA's interest deductibility limits links debt tax shields directly to corporate patenting activity.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's lineup leans into migration economics, but both papers offer useful lessons for applied econometricians—particularly around causal identification using linked administrative data and how to think about spillover effects that don't fit neatly into a regression framework.

The Returns to International Migration in a Free Mobility Setting: Evidence from Austria and Germany

Authors: Andrea Weber, David Card, Wolfgang Dauth, Wolfgang Frimmel, Julia Schmieder, Rudolf Winter-Ebmer

This is a nice case study in creative difference-in-differences design when you can't randomize migration decisions. The authors exploit linked Austrian-German administrative records to compare migrants against their own former co-workers—a comparison group chosen specifically to control for unobserved ability and firm-level shocks. The payoff: they find Austria-to-Germany migrants gain about 13% in daily wages, while the reverse flow gains only 4%, with the asymmetry traced to differences in average outcomes rather than selection effects. Why it matters: The co-worker comparison group is a portable identification trick for anyone studying individual-level transitions (job changes, relocations, promotions) where standard instruments are unavailable, and the 5-year bidirectional tracking design elegantly handles the thorny issue of return migration selection.

Migration, Identity and Political Economy in a Globalizing World

Authors: Vasiliki Fouka, Marco Tabellini

Less an empirical paper than a conceptual bridge-building exercise, this survey imports social psychology frameworks into economics to model how immigration reshapes self-identity and group categorization—and, in turn, political realignment. The authors argue this identity-based lens helps explain the rise of right-wing populism and the shift from economic to socio-cultural political cleavages in ways that standard economic-interest models struggle to capture. Why it matters: For econometricians working on political economy or discrimination, this is a useful reminder that identity is not merely a control variable—it's an outcome that itself responds to treatment (immigration exposure), which has implications for how we specify models of voting behavior and social preferences. The paper also flags open measurement questions: how do we operationalize "identity change" as an outcome variable that can be credibly estimated rather than just theorized about?

Worth a skim: Together these two papers make a compelling pairing—one shows the payoff of rigorous micro-level identification strategies using administrative data, while the other pushes back on the idea that migration's effects can be fully captured by wage and labor-market outcomes alone. If you work on treatment effect heterogeneity or spillovers, Fouka and Tabellini's discussion of "open questions" is worth reading as a research agenda-setting piece, even though it's not a traditional empirical contribution. Meanwhile, the Weber et al. paper's supplementary appendix (details available via the NBER working paper page) is likely to reward close reading for its treatment of attrition and re-migration selection bias—a persistent headache in any panel-based causal design involving mobility.
