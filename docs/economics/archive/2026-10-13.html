Top Papers This Week

Why Chip Know-How Doesn't Just "Spill Over" — It's Bought, Sold, and Guarded

Title: How Knowledge Crosses Borders: Evidence from Semiconductor Manufacturing | Authors: Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Réka Juhász, Giulia Lo Forte, Jeff Thurk

At the heart of the global chip war is an assumption that technological knowledge eventually diffuses to whoever wants it badly enough. This paper says otherwise: in semiconductor manufacturing, cross-border learning flows through deliberate market relationships — supply chain partnerships, engineer mobility within multinationals, and FDI — not anonymous spillovers. That means access to frontier chip technology is a choice variable governments and firms can restrict or cultivate, not a natural byproduct of proximity or trade. With CHIPS Act-style industrial policy now a global fixation, this is essential reading for anyone thinking about how (or whether) technology transfer to allies — or denial to rivals — actually works.

Congestion Pricing Critics May Be Missing Half the Story — Driver Diversity Is the Key

Title: Road Pricing under Heterogeneity | Authors: Itai Ater, Brad Ross, Adi Shany, Shoshana Vasserman

Using a remarkable two-year field experiment tracking nearly 10,000 drivers under usage-based congestion pricing, this paper shows that whether tolls actually improve welfare hinges on two overlooked factors: how much drivers differ in their preferences and exposure to peak congestion, and whether toll revenue gets recycled back to drivers. Rather than treating congestion pricing as a one-size-fits-all policy, the authors build a rich equilibrium model showing gains can be large or vanish entirely depending on these design choices. As cities from New York to London wrestle with political backlash over tolling, this gives policymakers a sharper, evidence-based playbook for getting the design right.

Macroeconomics

This week's NBER lineup explores how institutions—from courts to central banks to pension funds—shape markets and macro expectations in ways that challenge conventional models.

Do Judges Move Markets? Ideological Shifts in the Supreme Court and Macroeconomic Expectations

Authors: Thomas Drechsel, Naixin Zhang

Using the unexpected deaths of Supreme Court Justices as natural experiments, this paper shows that markets treat judicial ideology as macro-relevant information: a more conservative Court shift lowers inflation expectations, real activity expectations, and bond yields simultaneously, while a liberal shift does the opposite. The co-movement suggests investors see these shifts as demand shocks rather than supply shocks. Why it matters: It's a striking demonstration that financial markets price in the macroeconomic consequences of judicial—not just legislative or monetary—power.

The Impact of Central Bank Stock Purchases: Evidence from Discontinuities in Policy Rules

Authors: Masao Fukui, Masayuki Yagasaki

Exploiting a kink in the Bank of Japan's rule for triggering stock purchases, the authors find that in normal times these purchases move long-term interest rates but not stock prices—yet once yield curve control pins down rates, the same purchases instead boost stock prices substantially. This reversal contradicts the simple "inelastic markets" story but fits a general equilibrium model with endogenous rates. Why it matters: As more central banks experiment with asset purchases beyond bonds, this offers rare causal evidence on how such interventions actually transmit to prices.

When Cash Flows Turn Negative: Liquidity-Driven Selling by Pension Funds

Authors: Aleksandar Andonov, Kristy A. E. Jansen, Joshua Rauh

Using granular holdings data obtained via public records requests, this paper finds that U.S. public pension funds—long assumed to be stabilizing, patient capital—hold thin safe-asset buffers and respond to negative cash flows (as payouts now exceed contributions) by selling equities, often at inopportune times. Why it matters: As pension demographics mature, this reframes a major institutional investor class as a potential source of procyclical selling pressure rather than market stability.

Explaining Realized Returns: Roll's R2 40 Years Later

Authors: Bryan T. Kelly, Yinan Su, Chao Zhang

Revisiting Roll's famous puzzle that standard macro and cash-flow variables barely explain stock returns, the authors use generative AI to extract the actual reasons cited in Wall Street Journal market summaries. These AI-extracted narrative "reasons" explain 30–40% of daily and monthly return variation out-of-sample—far more than traditional variables—and map onto themes like monetary policy, sentiment, and geopolitics. Why it matters: This is a clever proof-of-concept for how LLMs can unlock qualitative, text-based explanations where conventional quantitative factors have failed for decades.

Survival versus Scale: The Effects of Critical Access Hospital Designation

Authors: Ian McCarthy, Katie Leinenbach

Using the staggered rollout of Critical Access Hospital status, the authors find that cost-based Medicare reimbursement improved rural hospital operating margins by 3-4 points, but came paired with meaningful cuts to beds, obstetric services, and nursing staff. Why it matters: A timely look at the tradeoffs embedded in rural health policy—financial survival may come at the cost of reduced local capacity.

Also worth a look: Fernando Alvarez and David Argente offer a theoretical deep dive into how strategic complementarities shape persistence in linear-quadratic mean field games, with applications to capital accumulation and pricing. And in development economics, Agüero et al. present experimental evidence that privacy-enhancing survey methods substantially improve the accuracy of self-reported intimate partner violence data—a methodological contribution with implications well beyond its immediate topic.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics papers share a common thread: how institutions and market design shape who gets access to opportunity—whether that's frontier technology, highway lanes, public capital, or a college seat.

Industrial Policy via the IPO Market

Authors: Zhiguo He, Yiming Qian, Xi Sun, Wang Renxuan

This paper shows that in China, government favor—not firm profitability—is the best predictor of which industries get to go public. Favored sectors saw 287% more IPOs and 148% higher proceeds than non-favored ones between 2002 and 2022, with the state controlling listing slots while markets pick winners within them. Why it matters: it offers a rare, quantified look at how authoritarian capital markets blend state control with market discipline, and the valuation premium attached to political favor raises questions about capital misallocation.

Road Pricing under Heterogeneity

Authors: Itai Ater, Brad Ross, Adi Shany, Shoshana Vasserman

Using a two-year field experiment with nearly 10,000 drivers, the authors show that congestion pricing's welfare gains hinge on driver heterogeneity and how toll revenue is returned to the public. Their model finds that a well-designed pricing scheme can generate welfare gains comparable to adding an entire new lane to every highway. Why it matters: this directly counters the common political argument that tolls simply redistribute costs without real efficiency gains.

How Knowledge Crosses Borders: Evidence from Semiconductor Manufacturing

Authors: Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Réka Juhász, Giulia Lo Forte, Jeff Thurk

Examining the chip industry, the authors find that cross-border technology diffusion happens mainly through deliberate market relationships—supply chains, multinational staffing, and FDI—rather than passive spillovers. This means access to frontier know-how can be deliberately granted or withheld by firms and governments. Why it matters: as countries race to build domestic chip capacity, this suggests industrial policy succeeds only when paired with continued international market access, not import substitution alone.

Affirmative Action in Centralized College Admissions Systems

Authors: Nano Barahona, Cauê Dobbin, Sebastián Otero

Studying Brazil's mandate to reserve half of federal university seats for disadvantaged students, the authors trace both direct effects and ripple effects as displaced students resettle elsewhere in the system. They find that graduation gains for beneficiary students are roughly double the losses experienced by marginally displaced students. Why it matters: this general-equilibrium approach offers a more complete welfare accounting than typical before/after comparisons, relevant to ongoing debates over affirmative action worldwide.

Gains from Trade in Gun Policy: A Compensation-Based Benchmark

Authors: Bradley Shapiro, Sara Drango, Sarah Moshary

Using stated-choice experiments, the authors estimate how much money it would take to convince handgun buyers to switch to long guns or forgo a purchase entirely. They find many buyers need surprisingly little compensation, making targeted buyouts a cost-effective way to reduce homicides relative to standard value-of-life estimates. Why it matters: this reframes gun policy away from bans and toward market-based incentives that might sidestep political and constitutional roadblocks.

Also worth a look: Sweeting, Tao & Wang model how private cost information drives signaling behavior in oligopoly quantity competition, applying their framework to the global oil market. Almazan et al. find that firms go public primarily to broaden and improve their access to debt financing, not just equity. And Anderson, Larch & Yotov present a clever "inverted gravity" method for backing out the trade cost changes needed to hit specific welfare targets, with applications to NAFTA.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics roundup features a notable methodological innovation for structural estimation, alongside an applied paper demonstrating creative identification strategies using occupational exposure data.

Indirect Variational Inference: Applications to Earnings Dynamics

Authors: Neele L. Balke, Stéphane Bonhomme, Thibaut Lamadon

Variational inference (VI) has become a workhorse tool in machine learning for approximating intractable likelihoods, but economists have been hesitant to adopt it because flexible variational families don't guarantee recovery of true structural parameters. This paper tackles that problem head-on by introducing "indirect variational inference" (IVI), which uses VI as an auxiliary model and then corrects for the bias it introduces—similar in spirit to indirect inference methods already familiar to structural econometricians. The authors demonstrate the approach using earnings dynamics models, showing that the choice of variational posterior matters a great deal, and that IVI can deliver consistent parameter estimates while retaining much of VI's computational speed. Why it matters: This could be a significant step toward making fast, scalable machine-learning estimation methods trustworthy for structural economic models with latent variables—a long-standing computational bottleneck in labor and macro-labor research.

Does Remote Work Increase Fertility?

Authors: Andrew C. Johnston

Rather than relying on occupation—which workers may have strategically switched into or out of after 2020—Johnston uses college major as a cleaner, pre-determined proxy for remote-work exposure, exploiting variation in how "teleworkable" different majors' typical jobs are. He finds that among married women, higher teleworkability actually reduced birth rates by about 2% per standard deviation, driven largely by a corresponding rise in full-time employment. No significant effects emerge for men or unmarried women. Why it matters: The identification strategy—using a fixed, pre-pandemic characteristic (college major) instead of a potentially endogenous one (occupation)—is a nice applied illustration of avoiding selection bias in natural experiments, and the substantive finding complicates the popular narrative that remote work is pro-natalist.

That's a shorter week for new econometrics methodology specifically, but both papers are worth a close read: one for anyone working with latent-variable or structural models looking for faster estimation tools, and the other as a case study in creative instrument/exposure construction for difference-in-differences style designs.
