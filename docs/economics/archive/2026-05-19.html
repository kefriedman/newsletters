Top Papers This Week

Remote Work's Hidden Cost: Why Junior Workers Are Losing the Learning Game

Title: The Power of Proximity to Coworkers | Authors: Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington, Amanda Pallais

Using data from a Fortune 500 tech company spanning the pandemic's office closures and return-to-office mandates, researchers found that sitting near teammates increases coding feedback by 18% and significantly improves code quality. The benefits are heavily concentrated among younger and less-tenured employees who are still building their skills. This provides the first rigorous evidence that remote work may be creating a "learning inequality" that could have long-term implications for career development and productivity growth.

The First "ChatGPT for Finance" Can Beat Human Portfolio Managers

Title: AlphaPortfolio: Goal-Oriented Investment Management Through Deep Reinforcement Learning | Authors: Lin William Cong, Ke Tang, Jingyuan Wang

Researchers have created what they call the first "large" generative AI model specifically for finance, using attention-based neural networks and reinforcement learning to construct investment portfolios. Unlike traditional robo-advisors that follow simple rules, AlphaPortfolio can handle complex, path-dependent investment objectives and process vast amounts of cross-asset information simultaneously. This breakthrough suggests AI could fundamentally reshape asset management by exploring investment strategies far beyond what human managers typically consider.

Why AI Won't Just Replace Workers—It Will Completely Reshape What Jobs Mean

Title: Endogenous Task Bundling, Skills and Automation | Authors: Joshua S. Gans

Most studies of AI's impact on wages assume jobs will keep the same bundle of tasks, but this research shows that's missing the real story. When automation breaks apart a job, firms decide how to recombine the surviving activities—and these decisions dramatically change which skills get rewarded. A skill that was worthless before AI could become the most valuable part of a post-AI salary, while previously essential skills might disappear entirely from the pay structure. This suggests we're not just facing job displacement, but a fundamental reimagining of how work itself is organized.

Macroeconomics

This week's macroeconomic research reveals fascinating insights into how human behavior and workplace dynamics shape economic outcomes. From the psychology driving our economic expectations to the evolving nature of learning in the modern workplace, these papers illuminate the human elements underlying broader economic trends.

The Psychology of Macroeconomic Expectations

Authors: Pedro Bordalo, Nicola Gennaioli, Florencio Lopez-de-Silanes, Simon G. Schröder, Andrei Shleifer, Maarten van Rooij

This groundbreaking study demonstrates that our personal experiences and emotions directly influence how we form expectations about inflation and economic conditions. By priming survey respondents to recall personal financial or health difficulties, researchers found that these memories systematically altered people's economic forecasts, making them recall similar experiences when predicting future macro states. Why it matters: This provides scientific evidence for Keynes' concept of "animal spirits" and suggests that macroeconomic expectations—which drive spending, investment, and policy decisions—are far more psychologically driven than traditional models assume.

The Power of Proximity to Coworkers

Authors: Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington, Amanda Pallais

Using data from software engineers at a Fortune 500 company during the pandemic and return-to-office periods, this study reveals that physical proximity to teammates increases coding feedback by 18.3% and improves code quality, with the largest benefits accruing to younger, less-experienced workers. However, experienced engineers actually write less code when sitting near teammates, suggesting they shift toward mentoring activities. Why it matters: As remote work becomes permanent for many organizations, these findings highlight important tradeoffs between productivity and human capital development that could have long-term implications for economic growth and wage inequality.

How do Workers Learn? Theory and Evidence on the Roots of Lifecycle Human Capital Accumulation

Authors: Xiao Ma, Alejandro Nakab, Daniela Vidart

This comprehensive study using German and US data shows that workers' learning sources evolve predictably over their careers: learning from coworkers decreases with experience while formal on-the-job training follows an inverted U-shape. The researchers' model reveals that remote work particularly disrupts early-career wage growth by limiting informal learning from colleagues, though formal training can partially compensate. Why it matters: This research provides crucial insights into how the shift toward remote work might affect long-term human capital accumulation and lifetime earnings, with potentially significant implications for productivity growth and income inequality.

Divergence in Climate Change Communication: LLM-Based Evidence from the IPCC and the Press

Authors: Sebastian Galiani, Franco Mettola La Giglia, Raul A. Sosa

Using large language models to analyze 114,000 matched claims from IPCC reports and major newspapers (1990-2023), this study finds that both scientific summaries and media coverage systematically emphasize the more severe end of climate projections while remaining within accepted scientific ranges. Surprisingly, both left- and right-leaning outlets show similar patterns in their coverage. Why it matters: This systematic bias in how climate information is communicated could influence public opinion, policy decisions, and economic planning in ways that may not align with the full spectrum of scientific evidence.

Also Worth Reading: "The Limits of Political Representation: Evidence from India" by Veda Narasimhan and Jeffrey Weaver examines 1.2 million hyperlocal representatives across rural India, finding that simply expanding political representation doesn't improve governance outcomes when representative capacity remains weak—a finding with implications for democratic institutions and development policy worldwide.

Microeconomics

This week brings fascinating insights into how institutions, markets, and policies shape economic outcomes—from the surprising benefits of public-private partnerships in healthcare to the hidden costs of market power in distressed lending.

Public Services Under Private Management

Authors: Maíra Coube, Luiz Felipe Fontes, Rudi Rocha

Brazil's innovative approach to hospital management—where private organizations run public hospitals while the state remains the financial owner—delivers impressive results. The study finds that this hybrid model increases hospital production and operational efficiency without compromising quality or equity, ultimately reducing local mortality rates by expanding access to care. Why it matters: This challenges conventional wisdom about public-private partnerships and offers a blueprint for healthcare reform that captures private sector efficiency while maintaining public oversight.

The Cost of Intermediary Market Power for Distressed Borrowers

Authors: Winston Wei Dou, Wei Wang, Wenyu Wang

When companies face financial distress, they desperately need capital to survive—but they're borrowing in markets dominated by a few specialized lenders with significant blocking power. Using sophisticated modeling of distressed lending markets, the researchers find that lender market power explains 50% of the exceptionally high spreads these borrowers pay, far beyond what credit risk alone would justify. Why it matters: This reveals how market concentration can exploit vulnerable borrowers at their most critical moments, potentially leading to inefficient business failures.

Global Policy Spillovers: How Environmental Policies Propagate through Product Attributes

Authors: Koichiro Ito, James M. Sallee, Jonathan (Andrew) Smith

When Japan implemented fuel-economy subsidies, something unexpected happened: car fuel efficiency improved not just in Japan, but also in the United States. The study introduces "attribute propagation"—the phenomenon where multinational firms harmonize product designs across markets, causing domestic policies to generate global spillovers. Why it matters: This finding suggests that unilateral environmental policies may be more powerful than previously thought, as they can indirectly improve outcomes in countries that haven't implemented similar measures.

Endogenous Task Bundling, Skills and Automation

Authors: Joshua S. Gans

As AI automates certain job tasks, the real action isn't just in which tasks disappear—it's in how companies choose to rebundle the remaining human activities. This research shows that standard wage impact studies miss this crucial dynamic, potentially even getting the direction of AI's effect wrong. Skills that were irrelevant before automation can become the primary wage drivers, while previously central skills may lose all market value. Why it matters: Understanding AI's true labor market impact requires looking beyond task replacement to how entire job structures evolve.

Econometrics & Methods

This week brings compelling evidence on the power of longitudinal analysis to uncover surprising null results and the critical importance of identifying exogenous variation in policy evaluation.

The Effects of GLP-1 Use on Mental Health, Self-rated Health, Employment and Marriage

Authors: Robert Kaestner, Cuiping Schiman

Using within-person variation from the rapid adoption of GLP-1 diabetes medications, this study finds no meaningful effects on mental health, self-rated health, employment, or marriage outcomes despite the drugs' well-documented weight loss benefits. The longitudinal regression approach cleverly exploits the recent diffusion of these medications to identify causal effects, revealing that even substantial physical health improvements may not translate into broader life outcomes. Why it matters: This challenges assumptions about the spillover effects of health interventions and demonstrates the importance of empirically testing seemingly obvious causal chains.

Valuing Disaster Prevention: Desert Locust Monitoring and Control

Authors: Joséphine Gantois, Anouch Missirian, Evelina Linnros, Anna Tompsett, Amir Jina, Gordon C. McCord, Eyal G. Frank

This innovative study tackles the classic identification problem in disaster prevention by exploiting conflict-induced interruptions to locust monitoring as exogenous variation in a natural experiment spanning three decades. The researchers trace how monitoring gaps in remote breeding areas lead to swarm outbreaks that reduce child height-for-age by over 7 percentage points in affected populations. Using this health impact alone, they estimate the monitoring system generates $25 billion in annual benefits, yielding an extraordinary benefit-cost ratio of 160:1 to 680:1. Why it matters: This provides rare causal evidence for the value of prevention programs and showcases how creative identification strategies can overcome endogeneity challenges in policy evaluation.

Both papers demonstrate sophisticated approaches to common econometric challenges. The GLP-1 study exemplifies how panel data methods can reveal counterintuitive findings that challenge conventional wisdom, while the locust monitoring research shows how researchers can extract causal inference from seemingly intractable identification problems by finding creative sources of exogenous variation. Together, they highlight the continued evolution of applied econometric methods in addressing complex policy questions.
