Top Papers This Week

Soak the Rich, Lose the Rich? What Actually Happens When Billionaires Flee Wealth Taxes

Title: Taxing Top Wealth: Migration Responses and Their Aggregate Economic Implications | Authors: Katrine Jakobsen, Henrik Kleven, Jonas Kolsrud, Camille Landais, et al.

Using rich Scandinavian administrative data, this paper delivers the most rigorous evidence yet on a question dominating tax policy debates worldwide: do wealth taxes drive the rich away? The answer is yes — a 1 percentage point hike in the top wealth tax rate shrinks the wealthy population by about 2% — and because many of these taxpayers are business owners, their firms suffer too when they leave. Yet the punchline is surprising: despite real migration responses, the aggregate fiscal and economic damage is modest, undercutting both the alarmist "millionaire exodus" narrative and the assumption that wealth taxes are fiscally costless. This is essential reading as countries from Norway to the U.S. debate wealth levies on the ultra-rich.

Poor Farmers Aren't Bad at Saving — They Just Forget What's Coming

Title: Retrieval Failures and Consumption Smoothing: A Field Experiment on Seasonal Poverty | Authors: Ned Augenblick, Brooke Kelsey Jack, Supreet Kaur, Felix Masiye, et al.

This QJE study overturns a core assumption in development economics: that poor households undersave because they lack information or willpower. Instead, Zambian farmers underestimate their own future spending by a staggering 50% — not because they don't know their expenses, but because they fail to recall them when making decisions. Remarkably, a simple, costless nudge — prompting people to mentally walk through upcoming expense categories — meaningfully improves recall and smooths consumption, with no new information provided at all. It's a striking demonstration that "retrieval failure," not irrationality or ignorance, may be a hidden driver of seasonal poverty — and that cheap behavioral fixes could rival costly transfer programs.

Macroeconomics

This week's papers span wealth taxation, oil market dynamics, sovereign debt theory, and the eye-watering economics of the AI infrastructure boom—offering a reminder that macro research increasingly bridges fiscal policy, finance, and real-world shocks reshaping the global economy.

Taxing Top Wealth: Migration Responses and Their Aggregate Economic Implications

Authors: Katrine Jakobsen, Henrik Kleven, Jonas Kolsrud, Camille Landais, et al.

Using rich Scandinavian administrative data, this AER paper finds that a 1 percentage point hike in top wealth tax rates drives a 2% decline in the stock of wealthy taxpayers through migration. Yet the aggregate economic fallout—on employment, investment, and value added—is surprisingly small. Why it matters: this offers some of the cleanest evidence yet that wealth taxes may be less economically damaging than feared, even accounting for elite flight.

Financing the AI Buildout

Authors: Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh

A staggering estimate: building 188 GW of U.S. AI data center capacity by 2032 will cost nearly $9 trillion, equivalent to 3.2% of GDP annually. As hyperscalers outspend their cash flows, financing is migrating toward leases, private credit, and securitization—vehicles that boost capacity but also hide leverage and concentration risk. Why it matters: this is a must-read for anyone tracking how AI capex could reshape credit markets and financial stability.

From Importer to Exporter: Oil Shocks and the U.S. Economy

Authors: Diego R. Känzig, James H. Stock, Luca Zanotti

The shale revolution flipped the U.S. from oil importer to exporter—and this flips the macro playbook too. The paper shows oil supply shocks that used to be contractionary have become expansionary, as favorable terms-of-trade effects now boost consumption, equities, and activity broadly. Why it matters: standard assumptions about oil shocks hurting the U.S. economy may need a rewrite.

Debt Sustainability and the Range of Multiple Equilibria

Authors: Olivier J. Blanchard, Pedro Martinez-Bruera, Gonzalo Huertas

Blanchard and co-authors tackle a classic sovereign debt puzzle: when can self-fulfilling default expectations create multiple equilibria? They show that higher uncertainty about fiscal fundamentals actually shrinks—and can eliminate—the range of multiplicity, making interest rates a smooth function of debt levels. Why it matters: this reframes how we think about debt crises and offers a more nuanced view of when markets might "panic" over sovereign debt.

Consumption, Savings, and Earnings Responses to Financial Windfalls

Authors: Rajashri Chakrabarti, Philippe d'Astous, Kory Kroft, Sheisha Kulkarni, et al.

Using Canadian lottery winner data, this study reveals stark heterogeneity in how windfalls are used: high-income winners save more and work less, while low-income winners spend more immediately. Why it matters: these marginal propensity to consume estimates have direct implications for stimulus design and targeting.

Also worth a look: Randomized to Opportunity uses Denmark's refugee dispersal policy to validate place-based opportunity measures with quasi-random assignment; The Investment Channel of Monetary Policy finds that only about 5% of firm-time observations show strong investment responses to monetary policy, challenging standard heterogeneity assumptions; and Which Workers Left the Labor Force Post-COVID digs into persistent labor force exits among low-skill workers, finding familiar explanations (stimulus, UI benefits) don't hold up.

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics roundup spans top journals and new NBER releases, with a strong theme of market design and information frictions — from how monopolists should segment consumers to how firms engineer their own risk exposure, and how AI itself can be modeled as a voting mechanism.

Screening and Segmenting: A Consumer Surplus Perspective

Authors: Dirk Bergemann, Tibor Heumann, Michael C. Wang

This AER paper tackles a classic question with a new twist: when a monopolist can adjust both prices and product quality across market segments, when does segmentation help versus hurt consumers? The authors show the consumer-optimal segmentation has an elegant structure — same-value consumers get the same quality everywhere, even as prices vary — and that the welfare verdict hinges on whether demand is more elastic than supply. Why it matters: this gives regulators a concrete, testable criterion for evaluating price discrimination and market segmentation policy.

Retrieval Failures and Consumption Smoothing: A Field Experiment on Seasonal Poverty

Authors: Ned Augenblick, Brooke Kelsey Jack, Supreet Kaur, Felix Masiye, et al.

Zambian farmers living off a single annual harvest dramatically underestimate their future spending needs — by about 50% — not because they lack information, but because they fail to retrieve it. A simple nudge asking people to think through future expenses by category boosted recalled expenses by 36-60% and reduced immediate spending. Why it matters: this is compelling evidence that "retrieval failure," not just lack of information, is a major driver of undersaving among the poor — suggesting cheap, scalable interventions could meaningfully improve financial resilience.

The Origin of Risk

Authors: Alexandr Kopytov, Mathieu Taschereau-Dumouchel, Zebang Xu

Why are some firms riskier than others, and why does risk correlate across the economy to generate aggregate volatility? This QJE paper models risk as a choice: firms select their productivity's mean, variance, and correlation with rivals. Larger, lower-markup firms turn out to be less volatile and less correlated with the aggregate — a pattern borne out in the data — and removing tax/markup distortions in a calibrated model substantially lowers GDP volatility. Why it matters: it reframes macro volatility as a byproduct of micro-level incentives and distortions, with direct policy implications for tax design.

A Model of the Attention Economy: Data, Platforms, and Discovery

Authors: Daniel Chen

A timely theoretical contribution modeling how platforms compete for consumer attention while monetizing it through targeted advertising. The model shows that data and interoperability policies can have effects that reverse over time, and that the celebrated "free" platform model can be inefficiently high or low in quality. Why it matters: offers a rigorous lens for ongoing debates over data regulation and the declining ad-revenue-to-GDP ratio despite the rise of data-rich advertising.

Social Push and the Direction of Innovation

Authors: Elias Einiö, Josh Feng, Xavier Jaravel

Using linked U.S. and Finnish data, the authors document striking homophily: innovators build products aimed at people like themselves in gender, class, and age — a pattern that's remained stable for decades. Embedding this "social push" into a growth model reveals large effects on cost-of-living inequality and long-run growth. Why it matters: unequal access to innovation careers isn't just a fairness issue — it actively shapes what gets invented and who benefits.

Also worth a look: Cornelißen, Dustmann & Schönberg (AER) disentangle knowledge spillovers from competition effects among coworkers; Gans (NBER) examines whether advertisers can detect "soft reserve" manipulation in second-price ad auctions à la the FTC's Amazon case; and Karapandža & Nyarko (NBER) offer a provocative framework showing LLMs behave like voting rules prone to Condorcet cycles — with real consequences for AI-generated financial advice.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics spotlight is all about getting your standard errors right—Cameron and Miller return with a major two-part guide to handling clustered and spatially correlated data, essential reading for any applied researcher. We also found a clever methodological aside in a prediction markets paper, plus several applied papers using strong identification strategies worth your attention.

Inference for Regression with Clustered or Spatially Correlated Data I: Framework and Clustering

Authors: A. Colin Cameron, Douglas L. Miller

The authors behind the classic 2015 "practitioner's guide" to clustering are back with an updated framework. This installment focuses on cluster-robust inference, with particular attention to finite-sample corrections needed when you don't have a large number of clusters—a perennial headache for researchers working with state-, industry-, or firm-level clustering. Why it matters: if your standard errors rely on asymptotic cluster-robust methods with a modest number of clusters, this is your updated playbook.

Inference for Regression with Clustered or Spatially Correlated Data II: Spatial Correlation

Authors: A. Colin Cameron, Douglas L. Miller

The companion piece tackles spatial correlation directly, covering the widely-used Conley (1999) spatial HAC estimator and—crucially—what to do when it breaks down under high spatial persistence. The paper also stresses the importance of correctly specifying spatial controls in the regression model itself, not just the error structure. Why it matters: as more applied work leans on geographic or network-based correlation structures, this provides a rigorous, practical roadmap for when standard spatial HAC isn't enough.

Are Prediction Markets Politically Biased?

Authors: Eric Zitzewitz

Using 145 years of prediction market data, Zitzewitz tests for systematic mispricing of candidates by ideology, gender, race, and age. He finds no evidence of politically motivated overpricing in election markets, though there's a small, statistically detectable left-leaning bias in short-term polling markets (likely driven by large sample sizes rather than economic significance). Why it matters: a nice empirical illustration of distinguishing statistical significance from economic significance in market efficiency tests—useful context as prediction markets gain policy relevance.

Racial Promotion Gaps

Authors: Deepak Hegde, Alexander P. Ljungqvist, Manav Raj

Exploiting the formulaic promotion rules at the US Patent Office, the authors show Black examiners face systematic promotion delays—even under Black supervisors—pointing toward belief-based rather than taste-based discrimination. The administrative data design, where promotion eligibility is observable and rule-based, offers a clean identification strategy for separating competing theories of discrimination. Why it matters: a rare setting where researchers can rule out statistical discrimination with accurate priors, sharpening the toolkit for studying labor market bias.

The Effects of Long-Term Exposure to Pollution on Health

Authors: Joshua S. Graff Zivin, Johnny Huynh, Timothy Frank Justicz, Adriana Lleras-Muney, Matthew J. Neidell

Using quasi-random assignment of Army duty stations as an instrument for PM2.5 exposure, this paper delivers some of the cleanest causal estimates yet of long-term pollution exposure on chronic disease in a healthy population. Why it matters: a strong example of leveraging institutional assignment mechanisms as instruments to overcome selection bias in environmental health research.

Also worth a look: The Kenya savings RCT offers a well-powered experimental design linking financial interventions to health behavior change, while the Tylenol recall natural experiment uses an interrupted time-series approach to test (and largely debunk) claims about prenatal acetaminophen and neurodevelopment.
