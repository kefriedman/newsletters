Top Papers This Week

Who Pays for Climate Policy? The Poor, It Turns Out—Twice Over

Title: The Unequal Economic Consequences of Carbon Pricing | Authors: Diego R. Känzig

As carbon markets expand globally, this paper delivers a sobering reality check: restrictive carbon policy shocks raise energy prices and cut emissions as intended, but they hit poorer households disproportionately hard—not just through higher energy bills, but through broader income and employment losses as the economy contracts. Using high-frequency data from Europe's carbon market, Känzig shows the "general equilibrium" pain is often invisible in simple energy-cost calculations but very real for low-income workers. This matters enormously for policymakers designing the next generation of climate policy: without complementary redistribution, carbon pricing risks becoming a regressive tax disguised as environmental virtue.

Lock Up the Phones: What Actually Happens When Schools Go Screen-Free

Title: The Effects of School Phone Bans: National Evidence from Lockable Pouches | Authors: Hunt Allcott, Jason Baron, Thomas Sean Dee, Angela Lee Duckworth, et al.

In one of the most comprehensive studies yet on a policy sweeping across American schools, researchers combine GPS data, teacher surveys, test scores, and sales records to measure what happens when students' phones get locked away during the school day. The pouches work—phone use drops substantially—but the short-run effects are messier than advocates might hope, with disciplinary incidents actually rising in year one. For a policy question currently being debated in statehouses nationwide, this is essential, rigorously-identified evidence on costs and benefits that goes well beyond anecdote.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro papers converge on a timely theme: inflation's drivers and dynamics, from why the post-pandemic surge happened to why the "last mile" of disinflation has been so stubborn—alongside fresh evidence on optimal monetary policy and the returns to public R&D investment.

The Inflation Attention Threshold and Inflation Surges

Authors: Oliver Pfäuti

Pfäuti finds that household attention to inflation roughly doubles once inflation crosses a 4% threshold, and that this heightened attention makes supply shocks more persistent and inflationary. This mechanism helps explain not only the recent inflation surge but also why disinflation's final stretch has dragged on so long. Why it matters: It suggests central banks face a nonlinear credibility problem—once inflation breaches a salience threshold, the costs of bringing it back down rise sharply.

Demand-Driven Inflation

Authors: Domenico Giannone, Giorgio Primiceri

Using structural VARs and a DSGE model, the authors argue that post-COVID inflation in the US and Euro Area was primarily driven by unexpectedly strong aggregate demand—fueled by pent-up consumption, aggressive fiscal stimulus, and loose monetary policy—rather than supply shocks alone. Why it matters: This reframes the inflation narrative: supply disruptions mattered, but it was excess demand that converted them into a full-blown inflation episode, with direct implications for how much credit (or blame) fiscal and monetary policy deserve.

The Returns to Government R&D: Evidence from US Appropriations Shocks

Authors: Andrew J. Fieldhouse, Karel Mertens

Exploiting a narrative classification of postwar R&D appropriations shocks, the authors estimate enormous returns to nondefense government R&D—140 to 210 percent—and find it accounts for roughly one-fifth of business-sector TFP growth since WWII. Why it matters: The findings imply the US has been significantly underfunding nondefense R&D, a timely point given ongoing debates over federal science budgets.

Aggregate-Surplus Maximizing Monetary Policy

Authors: David Baqaee, Ariel Burstein, Joao Guerreiro

This paper develops a HANK-based approach to optimal monetary policy that maximizes aggregate household surplus rather than imposing a specific social welfare function, sidestepping thorny distributional assumptions. The resulting quadratic loss function nests the standard New Keynesian objective and adds new terms for incomplete markets and heterogeneous labor wedges. Why it matters: It offers a rigorous, distribution-agnostic benchmark for monetary policy in heterogeneous-agent economies, clarifying when and how monetary policy can (or cannot) substitute for insurance.

The Effects of Monetary Policy on Macroeconomic Expectations: High-Frequency Evidence from Prediction Markets

Authors: Eric T. Swanson, Wang Renxuan, Yanbin Wu

Using high-frequency data from Kalshi prediction markets, the authors isolate how Fed rate decisions move market-implied expectations for output, employment, and inflation—shedding light on a long-standing puzzle where professional forecast revisions sometimes move the "wrong way" after rate hikes. Why it matters: This is a novel, promising data source for cleanly identifying monetary policy effects on expectations in real time.

Microeconomics

This week's microeconomics research spans labor market power, firm resilience to climate shocks, and the economics of institutional design—from union bargaining in Brazil to affirmative action in Indian universities and a new theoretical framework for taxing matched economic relationships.

Union Bargaining Power and the Amenity-Wage Trade-Off

Authors: Lorenzo Lagos

Using linked employer-employee data from Brazil and a clever court ruling that forced continuation of collective bargaining agreements, this paper shows that stronger unions raise both wages and non-wage amenities—without costing jobs. Strikingly, amenities account for 45% of the total compensation gains, suggesting collective bargaining can counteract employer monopsony power even as firms retain control over workforce composition. Why it matters: offers rare causal evidence that unions improve worker welfare on multiple margins simultaneously, challenging the view that labor protections always carry efficiency costs.

Firm Adaptation in Production Networks: Evidence from Extreme Weather Events in Pakistan

Authors: Clare Balboni, Johannes Boehm, Mazhar Waseem

Tracking firm-to-firm transactions in Pakistan, the authors find that flood-hit firms relocate and reroute their supply chains toward less flood-prone partners and roads—evidence that firms learn about climate risk through direct experience rather than knowing it upfront. A spatial model of production networks quantifies how this learning-driven adaptation will shape the economy-wide costs of more frequent climate disasters. Why it matters: suggests private adaptation can meaningfully blunt climate damages, but only after costly, belief-updating shocks.

Affirmative Action, Faculty Productivity and Caste Interactions: Evidence from Engineering Colleges in India

Authors: Robert W. Fairlie, Saurabh Khanna, Prashant Loyalka, Gagandeep S. Sachdeva

Studying India's mandate that roughly half of engineering faculty hires come from disadvantaged castes, this paper finds that reservation-category faculty have less education and experience—yet the paper digs into classroom-level productivity effects using random student assignment. Why it matters: provides rigorous micro-evidence on a question central to affirmative action debates worldwide—whether binding hiring quotas translate into productivity losses in practice.

A Beckerian Theory of Taxation

Authors: Margaret Chen, Mikhail Golosov, Ilia Krasikov

This theory paper extends optimal tax analysis to settings—like marriage markets—where both who matches with whom and how they split the gains from matching respond to taxes. The authors connect these equilibrium adjustments to Becker's classic insights on complementarity and sorting, offering a general toolkit for predicting how tax reforms ripple through matching markets. Why it matters: broadens public finance beyond standard labor supply models to any economic relationship involving sorting and bargaining—from marriage to business partnerships.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics lineup features a real-world field evaluation alongside a timely reassessment of one of the field's most debated methodological controversies—plus a reminder that beliefs don't always converge, even with shared information.

The Effects of School Phone Bans: National Evidence from Lockable Pouches

Authors: Hunt Allcott, Jason Baron, Thomas Sean Dee, Angela Lee Duckworth, et al.

Using a staggered difference-in-differences design and an impressively rich data stack—GPS pings, teacher surveys, test scores, and sales records—this QJE paper evaluates the real-world effects of lockable phone pouches in schools. The headline finding: pouches work as intended, meaningfully cutting phone use, but the rollout isn't painless—disciplinary incidents spike initially and student well-being dips before recovering. Why it matters: As phone bans sweep schools nationwide, this is some of the first large-scale causal evidence on what actually happens when policy meets adolescent smartphone habits—and it suggests patience pays off.

How Different are Different Differences-in-Differences? A Large-Scale Meta-Analysis

Authors: Peter Hull

In a clever use of AI-assisted replication, Hull reruns 1,346 staggered-adoption DiD specifications from 108 papers to test how much the "negative weights" problem—which launched a thousand new estimators—actually matters in practice. The answer: TWFE estimates correlate 91–98% with modern DiD estimands, and the odds of a statistically significant sign flip are under 2%. Why it matters: This is a notable corrective to years of methodological alarm-bells about TWFE, and it's also a nice proof-of-concept for AI-scaled empirical methodology research—expect more large-scale "meta-econometrics" like this.

Beyond the Numbers: Professional Forecasters' Narratives about Inflation and Stock Market Performance

Authors: Christian Conrad, Julius Theodor Schoelkopf, Michael Weber, Frank Brueckbauer

Why do professional forecasters disagree so much about markets even when they see the same data? This paper argues it comes down to the causal narratives they hold about how inflation affects stocks—narratives that shape not just forecasts but actual hypothetical portfolio choices. Critically, feeding forecasters identical signals doesn't resolve disagreement if they interpret the information through different stories. Why it matters: This challenges standard rational-expectations assumptions in macro-finance and offers a framework for thinking about persistent disagreement as a feature of belief formation, not just noise.

Also worth a look: Taken together, Hull's meta-analysis and the forecaster-narratives paper both push econometrics toward understanding heterogeneity—whether in estimator performance across hundreds of studies, or in belief formation across forecasters—rather than searching for one "true" number. A good week for methodological humility.
