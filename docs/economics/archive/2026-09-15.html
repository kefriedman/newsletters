Top Papers This Week

Robots vs. Workers: How Should We Tax an Automated Economy?

Title: Automation and Optimal Taxation: A Task-Based Theory | Authors: Henrik Kleven, Owen M. Zidar

As fears about AI and automation displacing workers dominate headlines, this paper delivers a rigorous theoretical answer to a pressing policy question: how should governments tax labor and capital when machines can replace workers in an expanding set of tasks? Kleven and Zidar show that even though the classic "sufficient statistics" formulas for optimal taxation still technically apply, automation dramatically changes optimal tax rates in practice because it alters equilibrium wages and capital returns. Calibrating their model to the U.S. wage distribution, they offer concrete guidance on how tax policy should adapt as machines climb further up the skill ladder. This is essential reading for anyone thinking about the tax code of the AI era.

California's $20 Fast-Food Wage: The Small Chains Are Paying the Price

Title: We've Got You Covered: Establishment-Level Evidence on California's Fast-Food Minimum Wage | Authors: Vitor C. Melo, David Neumark, Liam Sigaud, Gary Wagner

California's landmark $20 minimum wage for fast-food workers has been a flashpoint in the minimum wage debate, but prior studies using aggregate data couldn't cleanly identify which establishments were actually covered. Using granular establishment-level data, this study finds that the policy did reduce employment—but the pain is concentrated almost entirely among small chains, which also saw slower entry and faster exits. Large chains, meanwhile, largely absorbed the wage hike without major job losses. This nuanced finding reshapes the minimum-wage debate by showing that "who bears the cost" depends heavily on firm size and structure, with real implications for how similar policies should be designed elsewhere.

Macroeconomics

This week's papers venture beyond textbook macro into the empirical trenches — from minimum wage policy and school finance to insider trading by public officials — showing how careful micro-data work can answer big questions about growth, human capital, and institutional integrity.

We've Got You Covered: Establishment-Level Evidence on California's Fast-Food Minimum Wage

Authors: Vitor C. Melo, David Neumark, Liam Sigaud, Gary Wagner

Using establishment-level data to sidestep coverage and measurement problems in standard datasets, this paper finds California's $20 fast-food minimum wage had modest overall employment effects — but hit small chains hard, slowing their entry and increasing exits. Large chains, subject to the same wage floor, largely absorbed the cost without shedding jobs. Why it matters: minimum wage debates often treat "fast food" as monolithic, but this suggests scale and market power shape who bears adjustment costs.

The Macroeconomic Returns to School Finance Reforms: Micro-Calibrated Predictions Meet Causal Evidence

Authors: C. Kirabo Jackson

Jackson combines micro-level causal estimates of school finance reforms with a cohort-replacement simulation to trace how private earnings gains from education spending aggregate into macroeconomic output over time. The model predicts little movement for a decade or more, followed by gradual gains as better-funded cohorts enter the workforce. Why it matters: it offers a rare bridge between well-identified micro evidence and long-run growth accounting, suggesting patience — and sustained investment — pays off at the state level.

Measuring Racial Disparities in Police Stops: Evidence from Telemetric Mobility Data

Authors: Xiaofeng Gong, Yuling Han, Susan T. Parker, Matthew B. Ross, Stephen Ross

Using a novel two-stage correction method anchored in accident data, the authors show that raw telemetric mobility data badly understates minority driver presence on the road (15.2% vs. a corrected 28%). Once properly calibrated, disparities in Massachusetts State Police stops shrink to about half the size implied by uncalibrated data, though a meaningful 6.7 percentage-point gap remains. Why it matters: this is as much a methods paper as a policy one — it shows how mismeasured baselines can dramatically overstate (or understate) discrimination estimates in high-stakes empirical work.

Do Social Norms Substitute for Enforcement? Evidence from Public Officials' Home Purchases in Singapore

Authors: Tomasz Piskorski, Amit Seru, Jian Zhang, Chun Zhao

Even in Singapore — a global model of low corruption — the authors find civil servants disproportionately buy homes near future (unannounced) transit stations one to two years before public announcement, concentrated among mid-level officials in rail-adjacent agencies. This suggests that strong anti-corruption enforcement does not fully crowd out opportunistic behavior once information asymmetries exist. Why it matters: it raises questions about whether "clean" institutional reputations mask subtler forms of informational advantage-taking, with implications for how governance regimes are designed and audited.

Also worth a look: Randall Akee and Emilia Simeonova's study of economic heterogeneity among American Indians pushes back against one-size-fits-all narratives, emphasizing how self-governance and local context shape human capital outcomes — a useful complement to this week's broader theme of letting granular data challenge aggregate assumptions.

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics papers converge on a powerful theme: how policy design and market frictions shape who actually benefits from public spending, credit, and regulation—from disability payments to microloans to housing demolitions.

How Disability Benefits in Early Life Affect Adult Outcomes

Authors: Manasi Deshpande, Alessandra Voena, Jason B. Weitze

Using two quasi-experiments, the authors find that SSI benefits for children with disabilities help kids grow into more successful adults—but only when parents don't offset the transfer by cutting back their own work hours. When household income actually rises, outcomes improve; when parents substitute benefits for earnings, the effects disappear or turn negative. Why it matters: this suggests that for policymakers, how a transfer changes household behavior matters just as much as the transfer's size.

When Microenterprises Grow, Are Consumers Better Off?

Authors: Dean Karlan, Natalia Rigol, Benjamin N. Roth

A randomized trial of large loans to Chilean microenterprises finds that while borrowing firms saw profits jump 13.4%, their customers captured even bigger gains—an estimated $494/month in consumer surplus—with little evidence that firms grew merely by stealing business from competitors. Why it matters: this flips the usual microcredit evaluation on its head, showing that credit-fueled growth can be a genuine rising tide rather than a zero-sum reshuffling.

The Welfare Impacts of Urban Renewal and Forced Relocation

Authors: Milena Almagro, Eric Chyn, Bryan A. Stuart

Studying Chicago's HOPE VI demolitions, the authors build a neighborhood choice model showing that displaced public-housing residents suffered large welfare losses—only partially cushioned by vouchers—while gains from rising prices and neighborhood upgrading flowed mainly to higher-income homeowners. Even more generous vouchers and counseling can't fully close the gap, though expanded redevelopment could produce broader citywide benefits. Why it matters: a cautionary tale for cities pursuing redevelopment as a growth strategy without fully accounting for displacement costs.

A Sin Ban, Not a Sin Tax? Consumption and Incidence of SNAP Soda Restrictions

Authors: Crossan Cooper, Katja Seim, Jintaek Song

Analyzing the first statewide SNAP restrictions on soda and candy, the authors find an 11% drop in restricted spending and 5-8% reductions in calories and sugar, with retailers barely adjusting shelf prices. Notably, banning purchases achieves this at just 13% of the out-of-pocket burden that an equivalent soda tax would impose, while still generating an estimated $1.1 billion in annual welfare gains. Why it matters: this offers a compelling efficiency case for "sin bans" over "sin taxes" in means-tested programs.

Automation and Optimal Taxation: A Task-Based Theory

Authors: Henrik Kleven, Owen M. Zidar

This theoretical paper models optimal taxation when machines and workers compete for tasks, finding that automation is most intense around the 40th wage percentile—squarely hitting the middle class—which substantially reshapes optimal tax policy even though the underlying formulas retain their classical form. Why it matters: as automation anxiety grows, this offers a rigorous framework for thinking about how tax policy should respond.

Also worth a look: Jacob Moscona's Flowers of Invention traces how 1985 plant patent rights spurred crop innovation and raised yields; Nano Barahona and coauthors examine Chile's food labeling policy and its uneven effects across product categories; and Kay Jowers, Lala Ma, and Christopher Timmins investigate racial disparities in federal flood buyout payments and subsequent neighborhood outcomes.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's papers showcase econometrics in action—from novel identification strategies untangling causality in social and health outcomes, to a timely methodological reckoning with how AI is reshaping measurement itself in empirical economics.

The Measurement Revolution? Credible Measurement and Inference in the Age of AI

Authors: Melissa Dell, Ashesh Rambachan

This is essential reading for empirical researchers: as AI models make it cheap to turn text and images into structured data, the real challenge shifts from finding a scalable measure to choosing the right one among many plausible options. Dell and Rambachan lay out a framework—discovery, construct definition, and observation—for validating AI-generated variables rather than just asserting they work. Why it matters: as AI-derived measures proliferate in economics papers, this piece offers a much-needed roadmap for keeping inference credible.

Social Capital and Innovation: Evidence from Facebook Friendship Networks

Authors: Brad Cannon, David Hirshleifer, Joshua Thornton

Using massive Facebook friendship data, the authors show that "Economic Connectedness"—having friends with higher incomes—is strongly linked to local innovation, with a one standard deviation increase associated with 97% more patents per capita. They bolster the causal story with a clever identification strategy: a quasi-experiment exploiting fracking-driven income shocks to friends living elsewhere. Why it matters: this suggests financing frictions, not just idea generation, may be a key bottleneck for innovation in under-connected communities.

Immigration and Child Mortality during the Age of Mass Migration

Authors: Katherine Eriksson, Peter Zhixian Lin, Gregory Niemesh

Using a shift-share instrument built on historical settlement patterns, this paper examines whether immigrant-heavy neighborhoods raised mortality risk for the broader community in 19th-century Massachusetts—not just for immigrants themselves. The town-level panel data, spanning 1860–1915, lets the authors sidestep confounds that have hampered earlier work on this politically charged historical question. Why it matters: it speaks directly to long-standing narratives about immigration and public health with rigorous causal evidence rather than anecdote.

Did the Affordable Care Act Save Lives?

Authors: Anuj Gangopadhyaya, Cuiping Schiman, Robert Kaestner

Rather than relying solely on geographic variation in Medicaid expansion, the authors introduce a novel empirical approach to isolate the mortality effects of the ACA's non-Medicaid provisions (like marketplace subsidies) in states that didn't expand Medicaid. This fills an important gap, since prior research has focused almost exclusively on the Medicaid expansion piece. Why it matters: with ACA provisions facing ongoing political battles, understanding the full mortality impact—not just the Medicaid slice—is crucial for policy debates.
