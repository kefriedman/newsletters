Top Papers This Week

The ACA's Hidden Winners: Who Was Secretly Rejected by Insurers Before Obamacare?

Title: Health Insurance Underwriting and the Heterogeneous Effects of the Affordable Care Act | Authors: Pietro Tebaldi, Andrea Scalenghe, Joseph Orsini

Using a rare trove of pre-ACA insurance applications and underwriting files, this paper finally answers a question that has haunted health economists for over a decade: exactly who was being turned away by insurers before the ACA banned medical underwriting? The researchers can now precisely map how the law's ban on pre-existing condition exclusions—paired with new subsidies—created winners and losers across the age, income, and health-risk spectrum. This is a landmark contribution to understanding the largest U.S. welfare reform in half a century, moving the debate from theory to hard evidence on actual rejected applicants.

AI Made Patent Lawyers Better at Their Jobs — Even After the AI Was Taken Away

Title: Does AI Assistance Enhance or Erode Expertise? Evidence from a Three-Month Field Experiment in Patent Drafting | Authors: David Autor, Tanya Rodchenko, Josh Martin, Zanna Iscenko, Scott Strand, David Pearl, Melissa Ferere

In one of the most rigorous field experiments yet on AI and professional skill, 133 real patent lawyers at 11 U.S. law firms were randomly given a custom AI drafting assistant and tracked for three months. Rather than the "deskilling" many feared, blinded expert reviewers found lawyers' work improved significantly even 90 days later—when the AI was taken away. This directly challenges the popular narrative that AI tools erode professional judgment, offering some of the clearest causal evidence to date on how AI reshapes high-skill white-collar work.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro papers span the very small (pollution's effect on individual test scores) and the very large (fiscal financing choices behind decades of wealth concentration), with a common thread of using rich micro-data to answer classic macro questions about growth, distribution, and shocks.

Fiscal Policy and the Saving Glut of the Rich

Authors: Francesco Bianchi, Nicolò Ceneri, Leonardo Melosi, Alessandro T. Villa

This paper offers a compelling explanation for the decades-long rise in wealth concentration among the top 1%: a shift from inflation-financed redistribution in the 1960s-70s to debt-financed redistribution since the 1980s. In a New Keynesian model with progressive taxation, unfunded transfers generate inflation that erodes debt values, while debt-financed transfers instead let wealth accumulate among high earners who hold that debt. Why it matters: it reframes rising inequality as partly a byproduct of fiscal-monetary policy design, not just market forces.

Fairness Across the World

Authors: Ingvild Almås, Alexander W. Cappelen, Erik Ø. Sørensen, Bertil Tungodden

Drawing on a massive 65,000-person experiment across 60 countries, this study finds that how people perceive the source of inequality—merit versus luck—matters far more for their willingness to redistribute than the actual efficiency costs of redistribution. Globally, inequality from merit is tolerated 85% more than inequality from luck, dwarfing the effect of a 50% efficiency cost. Why it matters: this suggests redistribution policy debates should focus as much on perceived fairness narratives as on efficiency trade-offs.

The Allocative Cost of War

Authors: Marvin Amann, Yuriy Gorodnichenko, Oleksandr Talavera

Using detailed firm-level data from wartime Ukraine, the authors show that war's economic toll extends well beyond destroyed capital and labor—it also severely damages the efficiency with which surviving resources are allocated across firms. Comparing 2022 invasion effects to the 2014 conflict and the 2008 financial crisis helps isolate the roles of displacement, war intensity, and macro instability. Why it matters: postwar reconstruction strategies may need to prioritize restoring market efficiency and resource reallocation, not just rebuilding physical capital.

Air Pollution and Learning

Authors: A. Patrick Behrer, Joshua Goodman, J. Parker Goyer, R. Jisung Park

Analyzing nearly 10 million PSAT test-takers and using wildfire smoke as an instrument for pollution exposure, this paper finds that even moderate air pollution—well below EPA's historical thresholds—meaningfully reduces learning, cutting annual score gains by up to 19%. The effect is concentrated during the school year, pointing to classroom disruption as the mechanism. Why it matters: current air quality standards may be too lax to protect human capital accumulation, with long-run implications for growth.

Unemployment Insurance in Macroeconomic Stabilization with Imperfect Expectations

Authors: Bence Bardóczy, Joao Guerreiro

Using survey expectations data rather than assuming rational expectations, the authors show that UI extensions stimulate demand mainly by easing households' precautionary saving motives—but only if households actually anticipate future unemployment risk and benefit duration. Why it matters: this "expectations-driven" channel implies automatic stabilizers may be less automatic than assumed, with real-time communication and belief formation shaping their effectiveness.

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics roundup spans health insurance and workplace behavior to firm dynamics, with several papers using clever natural experiments and rich administrative data to challenge conventional wisdom about how markets and institutions actually function.

Health Insurance Underwriting and the Heterogeneous Effects of the Affordable Care Act

Authors: Pietro Tebaldi, Andrea Scalenghe, Joseph Orsini

Using rare proprietary data on pre-ACA insurance applications, this paper finally answers who actually benefited from the ACA's ban on medical underwriting—and it's not everyone equally. The gains were concentrated among low-income, high-risk, and older (50+) individuals, while others saw limited benefit. Why it matters: this offers the clearest picture yet of how the ACA's core protections were distributed, with direct relevance for ongoing debates about health insurance regulation.

Tracking Inequality: Teachers and the Allocation of Educational Opportunities

Authors: Michela Carlana, Francesca Miserocchi, Eleonora Patacchini

Combining administrative data, vignette experiments, and a field experiment, the authors show teachers are systematically less likely to recommend academically demanding tracks for disadvantaged students—even holding performance constant—driven by biased beliefs about these students' potential. Why it matters: this identifies a concrete, addressable mechanism perpetuating educational inequality, with clear policy implications for how track recommendations are made.

How Big is Small? The Economic Effects of Access to Small Business Government Support

Authors: J. David Brown, Matthew Denes, Ran Duchin, John Hackney

Expanding eligibility thresholds for small business programs sounds generous, but this paper finds it backfires: larger firms crowd out the smallest, most productive, and financially constrained firms, leading to more exits, lower wages, and less patenting. Why it matters: well-intentioned policy expansions can undermine the very dynamism and innovation they're meant to protect.

Meetings

Authors: David J. Deming, Katrine V. Løken, Alexander Willén, Yaling Xu

Using a novel survey linked to Norwegian administrative data, this paper delivers the first large-scale economic evidence on meetings—which eat up 12% of work hours and 14% of firm wage bills. Surprisingly, high-paying, high-revenue firms hold more meetings despite steeper opportunity costs, and meeting intensity correlates with faster wage growth. Why it matters: meetings may be a costly but productive coordination technology rather than pure waste, reshaping how we think about organizational design.

Is a Dollar a Dollar? How Transfer Design Shapes Household Spending

Authors: Therese Bonomo, Krista J. Ruffini, Diane Whitmore Schanzenbach

Exploiting pandemic-era variation across cash and in-kind, one-time and recurring transfers, this study finds household spending responses differ sharply by design—households spend more from in-kind and recurring transfers than from cash or lump-sum payments of equal value. Why it matters: this directly contradicts standard economic models where "a dollar is a dollar," with major implications for designing safety-net programs.

Also worth a look: Signals, Steps, and Setbacks documents age discrimination cutting both ways in China's job ladder (old applicants penalized for entry roles, young for management); Do Online Job Postings Capture Job Vacancies? warns that popular Burning Glass data overstate labor market concentration nearly threefold; and Behavioral Signatures of Cognitive Noise offers a compelling unified framework for many classic behavioral economics anomalies.

Econometrics & Methods

This week's econometrics roundup is headlined by a major methodological advance in regression discontinuity design—one of the workhorse tools of empirical economics—alongside two applied papers that showcase clever identification strategies for thorny causal inference problems in macroeconomics and political economy.

PLRD: Partially Linear Regression Discontinuity Inference

Authors: Aditya Ghosh, Guido Imbens, Stefan Wager

Regression discontinuity (RD) designs are everywhere in applied economics, but the authors show that standard approaches to constructing confidence intervals often perform poorly in practice—intervals can be needlessly wide or fail to achieve nominal coverage. They introduce a new "partially linear RD" estimator and stress-test it against twelve well-known RD applications, finding it consistently delivers shorter, valid confidence intervals than existing methods. Why it matters: Given how widely RD is used to estimate causal effects (from close elections to test-score cutoffs), a more reliable inference method could meaningfully change how confident we are in a large swath of published empirical findings.

Expectations-based Capital Inflow Shocks

Authors: Jongrim Ha, Dohan Kim, M. Ayhan Kose, Francis E. Warnock

Capital inflows are notoriously hard to study causally because they respond to expectations about future growth, creating classic reverse-causality problems. The authors solve this by using professional forecasters' expectations to strip out the anticipated component of capital flows, isolating genuine surprises across 27 emerging markets. They find these unexpected inflow shocks meaningfully boost output via consumption, investment, and easier financing—with equity inflows packing a bigger, more persistent punch than debt inflows. Why it matters: This "expectations-based" shock identification approach offers a clean, exportable template for isolating exogenous variation in other forward-looking macro variables.

An Empirically-Grounded Theory of Voting Based on Prosocial Motives

Authors: Dan M. Bernhardt, Laurent Bouton, Stefan Krasa, Francesco Squintani

While primarily a theory paper, this work is notable for econometrics readers because it explicitly builds its model to match known empirical regularities in turnout data—closeness of elections, ideological polarization, and voting costs. The authors derive testable predictions linking these primitives to turnout rates and winning margins, providing a structural framework that future empirical work can take directly to data. Why it matters: It's a nice example of theory disciplined by empirics, offering a menu of falsifiable predictions for political economists doing reduced-form or structural estimation on voting behavior.

Also worth a look: Even though this week's slate was light on pure methodology, both applied papers above are worth reading for their identification strategies alone—the expectations-purging approach in the capital flows paper and the empirically-calibrated theoretical model in the voting paper both illustrate broader trends in how economists are combining structural thinking with rigorous causal inference. If you work on RD designs in particular, the Ghosh-Imbens-Wager paper is a must-read this week.
