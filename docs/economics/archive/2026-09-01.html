Top Papers This Week

Trump-Era Tax Cuts Delivered Big Gains — Mostly to the Already-Rich

Title: Corporate Tax Cuts, Firm Growth, and Workers' Earnings | Authors: Patrick Kennedy, Christine L. Dobridge, Paul Landefeld, Jacob Mortenson

Using confidential employer-employee matched tax records, this study delivers the most rigorous evidence yet on how the largest corporate tax cut in U.S. history actually played out on the ground. Comparing similarly sized firms that faced different tax treatment due to their legal structure, the authors find real effects: more investment, higher sales, bigger payrolls. But the punchline is stark — 87% of the private income gains in the short run flowed to the top 10% of earners, with almost nothing trickling down to lower-paid workers. For a policy debate that's about to resurface with any future tax legislation, this is essential, hard evidence on who actually benefits from cutting corporate taxes.

When Hospitals Get Crowded, Black Patients Die More — White Patients Don't

Title: Rationing by Race | Authors: Manasvini Singh, Atheendar Venkataramani

This is a genuinely disturbing finding: analyzing 107,000 hospital admissions with precise timestamps, the authors show that as hospitals approach capacity, in-hospital mortality rises for Black patients but not for white patients — and it's not explained by who gets admitted. The mechanism is chillingly mundane: sicker Black patients wait longer for care than healthier white patients at nearly every capacity level, a pattern the authors trace using text analysis of clinical notes. As health systems everywhere grapple with capacity constraints and staffing shortages, this paper offers a rare, data-driven window into how scarcity can quietly encode racial bias into life-and-death outcomes.

Macroeconomics

This week's macro research spans trade policy, monetary-fiscal credibility, and tax design — with tariffs and central bank independence emerging as timely throughlines amid ongoing policy uncertainty.

Tariff-Dispersion Shocks: The Mean is the Variance

Authors: Stephanie Schmitt-Grohé, Martín Uribe

Using HTS 6-digit tariff data, the authors find that the average U.S. tariff rate and the dispersion of tariffs across goods move together almost in lockstep — driven by common shocks rather than independent "mean" or "variance" shocks. These combined shocks significantly move imports and the trade balance but have only muted effects on output. Why it matters: as tariff policy remains a live macro variable, this suggests trade-flow effects may be more predictable and separable from broader output effects than feared.

Accounting for Credibility: Fiscal-Monetary Interactions and the Credibility of Central Bank Mandates

Authors: Luigi Bocola, Gastón Chaumont, Alessandro Dovis, Rishabh Kirpalani

This paper models how central bank independence can endogenously break down: governments delegate monetary policy to inflation-targeting central banks, but face temptation to revoke that mandate when fiscal pressures mount. The economy can flip between "monetary-dominant" and "fiscal-dominant" regimes, generating sharply different inflation-debt dynamics. Why it matters: offers a rigorous framework for thinking about central bank credibility risk at a moment when political pressure on monetary authorities is rising globally.

This Time Is (Mostly) Different: U.S. Tightening Cycles and Global Resilience in the 1980s vs 2020s

Authors: Drishan Banerjee, Galina Hale, Harrison Shieh

Despite fears of a 1982-style emerging market debt crisis, the recent U.S. tightening cycle has not triggered a global recession. The authors show that greater fiscal space, more effective monetary policy, and reduced external vulnerabilities — not just flexible exchange rates — explain why emerging economies weathered this cycle better. Why it matters: provides a useful diagnostic framework for anticipating which countries are vulnerable in future tightening episodes.

The Environmental Bias of Corporate Income Taxation

Authors: Luigi Iovino, Thorsten Martin, Julien Sauvagnat

Carbon-intensive firms benefit disproportionately from the tax deductibility of debt, effectively lowering their tax burden relative to cleaner firms. The authors show that cutting corporate tax rates shifts investment toward clean firms, and that eliminating the debt tax shield could cut aggregate emissions without hurting GDP. Why it matters: a rare "free lunch" climate policy lever hiding inside routine corporate tax design.

Defying Distance? The Provision of Medical Services in the Digital Age

Authors: Amanda Dahlstrand

Using random assignment in Swedish online healthcare, this paper shows that better matching high-risk patients to effective physicians reduces both ER visits (-4.4%) and inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions (-3.1%) simultaneously. Why it matters: digital platforms may unlock efficiency gains in service industries once thought constrained by rigid, geography-based matching.

Also worth a look: Freedman and Neumark synthesize decades of evidence on place-based policy design; Sarah Turner examines how private lenders may fill the gap left by the elimination of GradPLUS graduate loans; and Cawley, Dreyer, and Simon find little evidence that the Navajo Nation's healthy-food tax policy improved birth outcomes.

Microeconomics

This week's Microeconomics papers span urban transit design, tax policy, neighborhood effects, and the economics of pollution regulation—with several offering surprising lessons about market failures and the limits of standard policy tools.

Optimal Public Transportation Networks: Evidence from the World's Largest Bus Rapid Transit System in Jakarta

Authors: Gabriel Kreindler, Arya Gaduh, Tilman Graff, Rema Hanna, et al.

Using Jakarta's massive bus network expansion, the authors find commuters care far more about wait times than time spent on the bus itself, and largely ignore the option of longer, indirect routes. Their model suggests a less centralized, more dispersed network design would boost both ridership and rider welfare. Why it matters: transit planners obsessed with hub-and-spoke efficiency may be missing what riders actually value.

Corporate Tax Cuts, Firm Growth, and Workers' Earnings

Authors: Patrick Kennedy, Christine L. Dobridge, Paul Landefeld, Jacob Mortenson

Exploiting the 2017 U.S. tax reform and matched employer-employee tax records, this paper shows corporate tax cuts did spur investment, sales, and employment—but the earnings gains were sharply skewed, with 87% of private income gains flowing to the top 10% of earners. Why it matters: even "successful" tax cuts that grow the pie can still leave most workers with crumbs.

Presidential Address: Neighborhood Effects and Missing Markets for Opportunity

Authors: Lawrence F. Katz

In his AEA presidential address, Katz synthesizes evidence that childhood neighborhoods causally shape adult outcomes, then diagnoses why markets fail to produce enough mixed-income neighborhoods—pointing to credit constraints, spillovers, behavioral biases, and search frictions. He argues customized mobility counseling paired with vouchers, plus place-based mixed-income policies, can help fix these missing markets. Why it matters: a sweeping agenda-setting piece for anyone working on housing, mobility, or opportunity policy.

Kumon Up: Private Tutoring Centers and Student Achievement

Authors: Joshua Goodman, Samuel Nitkin

Private tutoring chains disproportionately serve wealthy, Asian and White households, and a new chain opening raises district-wide test scores by 0.02–0.03 SDs—cheaply, relative to public spending—but widens racial achievement gaps since effects concentrate among White and Asian students. Why it matters: a low-cost lever for achievement comes with an equity cost that policymakers should anticipate.

Pollution Pricing in Equilibrium: Production, Reallocation, and Aggregate Impacts

Authors: R. Andrew Butters, Jackson Dorsey, Ivan Rudik

Studying 17 years of the EU Emissions Trading System, the authors find firms have little ability to cut emissions without cutting output in the short run—instead, nearly all short-run emissions reductions come from reallocating output toward cleaner firms, especially in power and cement. Why it matters: carbon pricing works, but mainly by picking winners and losers among firms rather than by greening production processes.

Also worth a look: Fear as a Campaign Tool shows Republican candidates strategically amplified Ebola fears for base signaling rather than reacting to real local risk; Firms as Electoral Monopsonies documents how dominant local employers shape workers' political beliefs and voting; and What Work Does Generative AI Do? offers the first task-level survey data on genAI adoption, finding usage is "widespread but shallow" across occupations.

Econometrics & Methods

It's a quieter week on the Econometrics front, but the single paper making waves this week is a heavyweight: a top-tier AER contribution tackling one of the thorniest problems in modern applied econometrics—what happens when the people being modeled start gaming the model.

Manipulation-Robust Prediction

Authors: Daniel Björkegren, Joshua E. Blumenstock, Samsun Knight

As machine learning algorithms increasingly determine who gets a loan, a job interview, or a government benefit, a familiar problem resurfaces in a new guise: Goodhart's Law. Once people know how a decision rule works, they have an incentive to change their behavior—not necessarily to become "better" risks or candidates, but simply to look better to the algorithm. This paper offers a rigorous, practical fix. The authors develop a method for building prediction models that explicitly account for strategic responses to the rule itself, producing "manipulation-robust" decision algorithms that remain stable and accurate even when fully disclosed to the people they evaluate.

What sets this paper apart is the empirical muscle behind the theory. Rather than confining the idea to simulations, the authors ran a large-scale field experiment in Kenya, deploying their strategy-robust linear models against standard approaches like LASSO in a live credit-scoring context. The manipulation-robust models held up better once real people had real incentives to try to influence their scores—suggesting that anticipating gaming behavior isn't just a theoretical nicety but a first-order concern for anyone deploying predictive algorithms in high-stakes, transparent settings.

Why it matters: As algorithmic decision-making expands into credit scoring, hiring, fraud detection, and public benefits allocation—often under legal requirements for transparency—this paper provides a template for building systems that don't collapse the moment people learn how they work. It's a rare case of theory, computational method, and field evidence converging on a genuinely actionable answer to an increasingly urgent policy problem.
